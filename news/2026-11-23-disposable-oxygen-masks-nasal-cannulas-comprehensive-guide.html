Disposable Oxygen Masks and Nasal Cannulas: A Complete Guide for Healthcare Procurement

A comprehensive guide to disposable oxygen masks and nasal cannulas for hospitals, emergency departments, and medical distributors. Compare oxygen delivery systems, flow rate specifications, material options, and procurement best practices for safe, effective supplemental oxygen therapy.

Supplemental oxygen therapy is one of the most common and essential interventions in modern healthcare. From emergency departments and intensive care units to general wards, prehospital ambulances, and home care settings, the ability to deliver controlled, reliable oxygen to patients with hypoxaemia or respiratory distress is a fundamental clinical requirement. At the heart of every oxygen therapy setup is the patient interface — the device that connects the oxygen supply to the patient's airway.

Nasal cannulas and oxygen masks are the two primary categories of disposable oxygen delivery interfaces. A nasal cannula is a lightweight, low-profile device with two prongs that sit in the nostrils, delivering low-flow oxygen at 1 to 6 litres per minute (L/min). An oxygen mask is a face-covering device that delivers oxygen over a broader flow range, with different mask types offering different levels of oxygen concentration control, from simple low-concentration delivery to high-concentration non-rebreather systems capable of delivering near-100% inspired oxygen.

The global oxygen therapy devices market continues to grow steadily, driven by the increasing prevalence of chronic respiratory diseases such as COPD and asthma, the expanding elderly population requiring long-term oxygen support, rising demand for home care oxygen therapy, and ongoing improvements in emergency and critical care infrastructure worldwide. For healthcare procurement teams, selecting the right mix of oxygen delivery devices — and sourcing them from a reliable, quality-certified manufacturer — is critical for patient safety, clinical effectiveness, and cost efficiency.

Nasal Cannulas

Design and Function

A nasal cannula is a simple, lightweight oxygen delivery device consisting of a soft, curved pair of nasal prongs connected to flexible supply tubing with a standard oxygen connector at the proximal end. The prongs rest comfortably inside the patient's nostrils, while the supply tubing loops over or behind the ears to secure the device in place. An adjustable sliding connector allows the fit to be customised for different head sizes.

Nasal cannulas are designed for low-flow oxygen delivery, typically at 1 to 6 L/min. At these flow rates, the delivered fraction of inspired oxygen (FiO2) ranges approximately from 24% to 44%, depending on the patient's respiratory pattern, tidal volume, and respiratory rate. Because the patient breathes ambient air between breaths, the actual FiO2 varies — nasal cannulas do not provide a precisely controlled oxygen concentration, but they are sufficient for the majority of patients requiring low to moderate supplemental oxygen.

Nasal cannulas are the most commonly used oxygen delivery device in healthcare settings worldwide. They are inexpensive, comfortable for extended wear, and allow patients to eat, drink, and speak normally during oxygen therapy.

Types of Nasal Cannulas

Type Characteristics Best For

Advantages and Limitations

Advantages: Nasal cannulas are lightweight and comfortable, allowing patients to eat, drink, talk, and move freely during oxygen therapy. They are inexpensive, easy to apply and remove, and well-tolerated for extended wear (many hours to days). They cause minimal skin irritation compared to face masks. They are the preferred device for long-term oxygen therapy in chronic conditions such as COPD.

Limitations: Nasal cannulas deliver a variable and imprecise FiO2 that depends on the patient's breathing pattern. They are limited to low-flow oxygen (standard cannulas up to 6 L/min; beyond this, the nasal mucosa becomes dry and irritated without humidification). They are unsuitable for patients with nasal obstruction, epistaxis, or facial trauma. At higher flow rates without humidification, they can cause significant nasal dryness, mucosal irritation, and epistaxis.

Simple Face Masks

Design and Function

A simple face mask (also called a Hudson mask or oxygen face mask) is a transparent, semi-rigid plastic mask that covers the patient's nose and mouth. Oxygen flows through a central inlet port into a small reservoir chamber inside the mask, and the patient inhales a mixture of oxygen and ambient air through perforations and open edges. Exhaled air exits through vent holes on the sides of the mask body.

Simple face masks operate at flow rates of 5 to 10 L/min and deliver an approximate FiO2 of 35% to 50%, depending on the flow rate and the patient's breathing pattern. The minimum flow rate of 5 L/min is important: flows below this level risk CO2 rebreathing within the mask, as the patient's exhaled air accumulates in the mask chamber instead of being flushed out. For this reason, simple face masks should never be used at flow rates below 5 L/min.

When to Use Simple Face Masks

Simple face masks are appropriate for patients who require a higher oxygen concentration than a nasal cannula can provide but do not need precisely controlled FiO2. They are commonly used in emergency departments, ambulance services, and general wards for short-term oxygen therapy. They are particularly useful when a patient cannot tolerate a nasal cannula due to nasal congestion, or when a higher oxygen concentration is needed temporarily while awaiting further assessment.

The mask should fit snugly but comfortably over the nose and mouth. Most simple face masks feature an adjustable metal or plastic nose bridge strip that can be moulded to the patient's nasal contour for a better seal. Excessive tightness causes discomfort and skin pressure marks; too loose a fit allows too much ambient air dilution and reduces the effective FiO2.

Non-Rebreather Masks (NRB Masks)

Design and Function

A non-rebreather mask is the highest-concentration oxygen delivery device available without mechanical ventilation or high-flow systems. It consists of a face mask body connected to an attached reservoir bag (typically 600-1000 mL in adult sizes) via a one-way valve at the bag outlet. The one-way valve ensures that the patient inhales only from the oxygen-filled reservoir bag and does not rebreathe exhaled air. Additional one-way valves on the mask exhalation ports prevent ambient air from entering during inhalation, further maximising the delivered oxygen concentration.

Non-rebreather masks require a flow rate of 10 to 15 L/min to keep the reservoir bag inflated. At these flow rates, they can deliver FiO2 values of 60% to 90%, approaching the theoretical maximum of 100% if all valves are functioning properly and the mask fits well. In practice, the effective FiO2 is typically 70-85% due to minor mask leaks and imperfect valve sealing.

Clinical Applications

Non-rebreather masks are the standard of care for emergency oxygen delivery in patients with severe hypoxaemia, carbon monoxide poisoning, shock, major trauma, and acute respiratory distress. They are a standard component of every emergency response kit, ambulance oxygen pack, and hospital crash trolley. Procurement teams should ensure that non-rebreather masks are stocked in adult and paediatric sizes, with adequate supply for high-volume emergency departments.

Procurement tip: Always stock non-rebreather masks with pre-attached reservoir bags and functioning one-way valves. Test a sample from each batch to verify that the one-way valves open and close correctly. A non-rebreather mask with faulty valves becomes a simple face mask, delivering significantly lower oxygen concentrations than expected — a potentially dangerous situation in critical care.

Venturi Masks

Design and Function

A Venturi mask (also called an air-entrainment mask or high-flow oxygen mask) is a precisely calibrated oxygen delivery device that uses the Venturi principle to entrain a specific ratio of ambient air into the oxygen stream, producing a fixed and predictable FiO2 regardless of the patient's breathing pattern. The mask features a colour-coded adapter with a precisely sized air-entrainment orifice. Each adapter corresponds to a specific oxygen concentration.

Orange 31% 6 ~52
Yellow 35% 8 ~60

Green 60% 15 ~60

Venturi masks are the oxygen delivery device of choice for patients who require precisely controlled oxygen concentrations, particularly patients with chronic hypercapnic respiratory failure (such as COPD exacerbations) where uncontrolled high-flow oxygen can suppress the respiratory drive and worsen hypercapnia. In these patients, starting with a Venturi mask at 24% or 28% FiO2 and titrating upward based on arterial blood gas (ABG) results is the standard clinical approach.

Most Venturi mask systems use interchangeable colour-coded adapters that allow clinicians to change the FiO2 without replacing the entire mask. Some systems feature a dial-adjustable Venturi adapter that allows continuous FiO2 adjustment from 24% to 50% with a single device, reducing the need to stock multiple colour-coded adapters.

Material Selection

The materials used in oxygen delivery interfaces directly affect patient comfort, device performance, and safety. Understanding material properties is essential for procurement decisions.

Component Material Characteristics
Mask body / prongs Medical-grade PVC or polyethylene Transparent PVC allows visual monitoring of condensation and patient secretions; soft, flexible materials reduce skin irritation; non-toxic and biocompatible for patient contact
Supply tubing Medical-grade PVC with corrugated or smooth bore Standard 2.1-metre length for most applications; kink-resistant design maintains flow; crush-resistant tubing for high-flow applications; oxygen-compatible materials that do not degrade or off-gas
Reservoir bag (NRB) Medical-grade PVC or latex-free rubber Collapsible design allows visual confirmation of oxygen flow and bag filling; must maintain integrity under repeated inflation-deflation cycles; opaque bags allow condensation monitoring without full transparency
Nose bridge strip Soft aluminium or plastic Mouldable aluminium provides the best fit and seal; plastic nose bridges are softer but less precisely adjustable; padded nose bridge strips reduce skin pressure marks
Valves (NRB/Venturi) Medical-grade silicone or PVC film One-way valves must open and close reliably with minimal resistance; silicone valves tend to be more durable and less prone to sticking; verify valve function on sample testing

All patient-contacting components should be latex-free and DEHP-free. Single-use disposable devices eliminate the need for reprocessing and reduce the risk of cross-contamination between patients. The entire assembly — mask or cannula, tubing, and connector — should be individually packaged in a clean, protective pouch.

Oxygen Tubing Specifications

The supply tubing that connects the oxygen source to the patient interface is an often-overlooked component that significantly affects oxygen delivery performance. Poor-quality tubing can kink, collapse, or develop micro-cracks that cause oxygen leaks and reduce the effective flow rate delivered to the patient.

Standard oxygen tubing: Typically 2.1 metres (7 feet) in length, with a standard 15 mm or 22 mm connector that fits most oxygen flowmeter outlets and concentrator ports. Smooth-bore tubing is suitable for low-flow applications. Corrugated or reinforced-bore tubing provides better kink resistance and is preferred for higher flow rates and mobile use.

Star-lumen tubing: Features a star-shaped internal cross-section that provides superior kink resistance compared to standard round-bore tubing. The star shape allows the tubing to bend and flex without collapsing, maintaining consistent oxygen flow even when the tubing is compressed or bent sharply. This design is particularly valuable for patients who are mobile or restless during oxygen therapy.

Crush-resistant tubing: Features an internal or external reinforcement layer (typically a spiral wire or polymer ridge) that prevents the tubing from being permanently flattened if stepped on or compressed by hospital bed rails or wheelchair wheels. This is essential for ambulance, transport, and ward use where tubing is at risk of being crushed.

Procurement tip: Specify kink-resistant or crush-resistant tubing for ambulance, emergency, and transport applications. For general ward use, standard smooth-bore tubing is adequate. Always verify that the tubing connector matches your oxygen supply system (15 mm standard connector is most common).

Quality Evaluation Checklist

When assessing oxygen mask and nasal cannula suppliers, evaluate product quality using the following criteria:

  • Prong softness (cannulas): Nasal prongs should be soft and flexible, with smooth edges that do not cause mucosal irritation. Press the prongs between your fingers — they should compress easily and rebound without permanent deformation.
  • Mask transparency: Transparent mask bodies allow visual monitoring of condensation levels, patient secretions, and skin contact points. Verify that the material is clear, not cloudy or discoloured.
  • Valve function (NRB masks): Test the one-way valves on a sample from each batch. The inhalation valve should open easily under gentle negative pressure and close completely at rest. The exhalation valve(s) should allow free exhalation but prevent ambient air ingress during inhalation.
  • Reservoir bag integrity: Inflate the reservoir bag by hand to its normal operating capacity. It should inflate smoothly without leaks and deflate uniformly. Check for weak points, thin spots, or manufacturing defects in the seal area.
  • Nose bridge adjustability: Test the nose bridge strip by bending it to shape and releasing it. It should hold the moulded shape without springing back. Aluminium nose bridges hold shape better than plastic alternatives.
  • Tubing kink resistance: Bend a sample of supply tubing to a tight radius. It should not kink or collapse, and airflow should remain unobstructed. For crush-resistant tubing, apply gentle compressive force and verify that the tubing rebounds.
  • Connector fit: Verify that the oxygen tubing connector fits securely onto your standard oxygen flowmeter outlet without excessive force or loose fit. A proper connection should produce an audible click or friction seal that does not disconnect under gentle pulling.
  • Individual packaging: Each device should be individually packaged in a clean, protective pouch with product name, size, lot number, manufacturing date, and expiry date clearly printed. Packaging should be intact and free of tears or contamination.
  • Latex-free and DEHP-free confirmation: Verify that product documentation confirms the absence of latex and DEHP. This is essential for patient safety, particularly in facilities serving latex-sensitive patient populations.

Clinical Applications

Emergency Medicine and Prehospital Care

In emergency settings, oxygen delivery begins immediately on patient assessment. Non-rebreather masks are the standard initial device for patients with severe hypoxaemia, respiratory distress, or shock. Nasal cannulas are used for stable patients requiring low-flow oxygen, or for long-term oxygen therapy during patient transport. Ambulance oxygen kits should carry both device types in adult and paediatric sizes, along with appropriate tubing lengths.

General Ward and Long-Term Oxygen Therapy

Nasal cannulas are the dominant device for general ward oxygen therapy due to their comfort and cost-effectiveness. Patients requiring moderate oxygen concentrations may be fitted with simple face masks. For patients with COPD or other conditions requiring controlled oxygen, Venturi masks are the standard of care. Long-term oxygen therapy patients (typically 15+ hours per day) almost exclusively use nasal cannulas due to their comfort during extended wear.

Intensive Care and High-Dependency Units

ICU patients may use any of the oxygen delivery devices described above, depending on their clinical status. High-flow nasal cannula (HFNC) systems have become increasingly common in ICUs as a non-invasive alternative to mechanical ventilation for patients with acute hypoxaemic respiratory failure. HFNC delivers heated, humidified oxygen at flow rates up to 60 L/min with precise FiO2 control from 21% to 100%, providing both oxygenation and a mild positive airway pressure effect.

Home Care

Nasal cannulas are the standard home oxygen delivery device, used with either stationary oxygen concentrators or portable oxygen cylinders. Patients on long-term oxygen therapy require a steady supply of replacement cannulas (typically changed every 2-4 weeks for hygiene reasons). Home care oxygen tubing is often supplied in longer lengths (4-5 metres) to allow patient mobility within the home environment.

Regulatory Standards and Compliance

ISO 13485 — Quality Management

Manufacturers operating under an ISO 13485 certified quality management system demonstrate consistent control over raw material selection, injection moulding and extrusion process parameters, dimensional inspection, finished product testing, batch traceability, and packaging integrity. For oxygen delivery devices, this includes verification of flow rate accuracy, connector dimensional compliance, oxygen concentration specifications (for Venturi masks), and biocompatibility of patient-contacting materials.

ISO 10993 — Biocompatibility

Oxygen masks and nasal cannulas contact skin and mucosal tissue and should comply with relevant ISO 10993 biocompatibility requirements. Testing typically includes cytotoxicity, sensitisation, and irritation assessments. All patient-contacting materials should be latex-free and DEHP-free.

ISO 7765 — Oxygen Therapy Connectors

Oxygen therapy equipment connectors should comply with relevant dimensional and performance standards to ensure interchangeability between devices from different manufacturers. The standard 15 mm and 22 mm connector sizes ensure that oxygen tubing from one manufacturer connects properly to flowmeters, concentrators, and wall outlets from another.

CE Marking and FDA Registration

Oxygen masks and nasal cannulas are classified as medical devices in most regulatory jurisdictions. In the European Union, they are regulated under the Medical Device Regulation (MDR) and require CE marking. In the United States, they are generally Class I devices subject to general controls and establishment registration. Procurement teams serving regulated markets should verify the manufacturer's registration status and conformity assessment documentation.

Procurement Best Practices

Stock the Right Mix

Not every oxygen delivery device is needed in every department, but a well-equipped healthcare facility should maintain an appropriate mix. General wards need primarily nasal cannulas and simple face masks. Emergency departments and ambulance services need the full range: nasal cannulas, simple face masks, non-rebreather masks, and Venturi masks. Paediatric areas need smaller sizes of all devices. ICUs may additionally need high-flow nasal cannula systems.

Specify Tubing Type by Use Case

Standard smooth-bore tubing is adequate for general ward and home care use. For ambulance, emergency, and transport applications, specify kink-resistant or crush-resistant tubing. This prevents oxygen flow interruption when tubing is compressed by stretcher rails, wheelchair wheels, or during patient transport — a scenario that can cause dangerous drops in delivered oxygen concentration.

Maintain High Safety Stock

Oxygen delivery devices are low-cost, high-usage consumables. Running out of any size or type is unacceptable, particularly for emergency devices like non-rebreather masks. Maintain a minimum of 8-12 weeks of safety stock for all device types, with higher reserves for the most commonly used items (standard nasal cannulas, adult non-rebreather masks, and 2.1-metre oxygen tubing).

Verify Valve Function on NRB Masks

Non-rebreather masks are only as reliable as their one-way valves. A mask with a stuck or leaking inhalation valve delivers significantly less oxygen than specified. Include valve function testing in your incoming quality inspection protocol. Test a statistically significant sample from each incoming batch to verify that all valves open and close correctly.

Storage and Handling

Store oxygen delivery devices in their original packaging in a clean, dry environment away from direct sunlight, extreme temperatures, and chemical vapours. PVC components can become brittle with prolonged UV exposure. Implement FIFO (first in, first out) stock rotation. Do not store oxygen devices near heat sources or flammable materials. Individually packaged devices should be inspected before use to confirm packaging integrity and verify the expiry date has not passed.

Environmental Considerations

As single-use medical devices made primarily from PVC, disposable oxygen masks and nasal cannulas contribute to medical plastic waste. Procurement teams can reduce environmental impact by selecting manufacturers who minimise packaging materials, use recyclable outer packaging, and implement lean manufacturing practices. While the clinical-contact device itself must be disposed of through clinical waste streams after use, selecting suppliers committed to sustainable manufacturing and offering take-back programmes for clean outer packaging is one way to address environmental concerns without compromising patient safety.
