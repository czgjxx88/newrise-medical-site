Disposable Urinary Catheters: Types, Sizing, and Clinical Procurement Guide

Disposable urinary catheters are among the most frequently used medical devices in hospitals, long-term care facilities, and home healthcare settings worldwide. This guide covers catheter types, sizing systems, material selection, infection prevention strategies, and practical procurement considerations for healthcare organisations and medical supply distributors.

Urinary catheterisation is one of the most common clinical procedures performed in healthcare settings globally. An estimated fifteen to twenty-five per cent of hospitalised patients receive a urinary catheter at some point during their stay, making urinary catheters one of the highest-volume consumable medical devices in clinical supply chains. The global urinary catheter market exceeds five billion US dollars and continues to grow, driven by an ageing population, rising rates of urinary retention and incontinence, expanded surgical volumes, and growing adoption of catheterisation in home care and long-term care settings.

For procurement managers, clinical supply coordinators, and medical device distributors, selecting the right urinary catheter products involves balancing clinical requirements, patient comfort, infection risk, and total supply chain cost. An inappropriate catheter choice — whether in material, size, balloon volume, or drainage configuration — can lead to complications including catheter-associated urinary tract infections (CAUTIs), urethral trauma, encrustation and blockage, and extended hospital stays. This guide provides a comprehensive overview of disposable urinary catheter types, sizing conventions, material properties, infection prevention best practices, and procurement strategies.

Types of Disposable Urinary Catheters

Indwelling (Foley) Catheters

Foley catheters, also known as indwelling urinary catheters, are the most widely used type of urinary catheter in hospital and long-term care settings. They feature an inflatable retention balloon near the distal tip that, once inflated inside the bladder, holds the catheter securely in place without the need for external fixation. Foley catheters connect to a closed drainage bag system, allowing continuous bladder drainage over periods ranging from hours to several weeks.

Foley catheters are indicated for surgical procedures requiring precise urinary output monitoring, acute urinary retention, critical care fluid balance management, palliative care for patients with end-of-life incontinence, and management of open sacral or perineal wounds in incontinent patients. Standard Foley catheters have two lumens: one for urine drainage and one for balloon inflation. Three-way Foley catheters include a third lumen for continuous bladder irrigation (CBI), commonly used after urological surgery to prevent clot formation and maintain catheter patency.

Intermittent (Straight) Catheters

Intermittent catheters, also called "in-and-out" or straight catheters, are inserted through the urethra to empty the bladder and then removed immediately. They do not have a retention balloon and are not designed for continuous indwelling use. Intermittent catheterisation is the preferred method for patients with chronic urinary retention who can self-catheterise or receive periodic catheterisation from a caregiver, as it carries a significantly lower risk of urinary tract infection compared to indwelling catheterisation.

Modern intermittent catheters are available in several formats: uncoated standard straight catheters, hydrophilic-coated catheters that become slippery when activated with water or saline (reducing urethral friction and trauma), pre-lubricated catheters with integrated lubricant in the packaging for convenience, and compact catheter systems with integrated collection bags designed for discreet use outside the home. Hydrophilic catheters have gained significant clinical adoption due to their demonstrated reduction in urethral microtrauma, patient-reported comfort, and lower incidence of bacteriuria in long-term intermittent catheterisation programmes.

External (Condom) Catheters

External urinary catheters, commonly known as condom catheters or Texas catheters, are non-invasive devices that fit over the penis and connect to a drainage bag. They are used exclusively for male patients with urinary incontinence who can void spontaneously but require continence management. External catheters eliminate the infection risk associated with urethral catheterisation because they do not breach the urinary tract.

External catheters are available in adhesive and non-adhesive (self-gripping) variants. Adhesive condom catheters feature an internal adhesive coating that secures the device to the skin, providing reliable retention over extended wear periods. Non-adhesive versions use a soft foam or silicone roll-on design that grips the penis without glue, making them suitable for patients with sensitive skin or adhesive allergies. External catheters are an important component of incontinence care programmes, particularly in nursing homes and long-term care facilities where resident dignity and skin integrity are priorities.

Suprapubic Catheters

Suprapubic catheters are inserted through the abdominal wall directly into the bladder via a surgically created stoma. While the initial insertion is a surgical procedure, the catheters used for ongoing suprapubic drainage are disposable replacement catheters — typically Foley-style catheters with an inflatable balloon. Suprapubic catheterisation is preferred when urethral catheterisation is contraindicated (due to urethral stricture, trauma, or prostate obstruction) or for patients requiring long-term catheterisation who benefit from the easier hygiene access and reduced urethral complications that suprapubic drainage provides.

Catheter Materials: Selection and Clinical Implications

The material from which a urinary catheter is manufactured significantly affects its clinical performance, patient comfort, safe indwelling duration, and resistance to encrustation.

Material Typical Use Max Duration Key Properties
PVC (polyvinyl chloride) Short-term intermittent Single use Cost-effective, rigid structure, smooth surface
Latex Short- to medium-term indwelling Up to 7 days Flexible, comfortable, economical; contraindicated for latex-allergic patients
Silicone-coated latex Medium-term indwelling Up to 14 days Latex core with silicone coating for reduced friction and encrustation
100% silicone Medium- to long-term indwelling Up to 28 days Excellent biocompatibility, wide lumen for a given French size, low encrustation
Hydrogel-coated Intermittent and indwelling Varies Hydrophilic coating reduces friction, improves patient comfort, lowers bacteriuria risk
Silicone elastomer Suprapubic and long-term Up to 28 days Soft, flexible, high biocompatibility, large drainage eyes

Silicone catheters offer a notably larger internal lumen compared to latex catheters of the same French size, because silicone walls can be manufactured thinner while maintaining structural integrity. This means a 14 Fr silicone catheter can provide drainage equivalent to a 16 Fr latex catheter, offering the clinical benefit of a larger drainage channel without increasing urethral diameter and patient discomfort. For patients requiring long-term indwelling catheterisation, 100% silicone is generally preferred because of its superior biocompatibility, reduced encrustation rate, and lower incidence of allergic reactions compared to latex.

Procurement tip: Stock both latex and silicone Foley catheters in your formulary. Use latex for short-term, cost-sensitive applications where the patient has no latex sensitivity. Reserve silicone for patients with known latex allergy, long-term catheterisation needs, and patients with a history of catheter encrustation. This tiered approach optimises both clinical outcomes and supply budget.

Catheter Sizing: The French Scale

Urinary catheter size is measured on the French (Fr or Ch) scale, where one French unit equals one-third of a millimetre in external diameter. A 14 Fr catheter has an external diameter of approximately 4.67 mm, while an 18 Fr catheter measures approximately 6.0 mm. Selecting the correct catheter size is a critical clinical decision with direct implications for patient comfort, drainage efficiency, and complication risk.

The general principle is to use the smallest catheter size that adequately drains the bladder. Oversized catheters increase urethral trauma, patient discomfort, and the risk of urethral stricture. Undersized catheters may kink, drain poorly, or allow urine to bypass the catheter alongside it. Standard adult Foley catheter sizes range from 12 Fr to 18 Fr, with 14 Fr and 16 Fr being the most commonly used sizes for general adult patients. Larger sizes (18 Fr to 24 Fr) may be required for patients with haematuria (blood clots can obstruct smaller lumens) or after urological surgery where irrigation flow is needed.

Balloon Volume Selection

Foley catheter balloons are available in standard volumes, and the balloon size must match the clinical application:

  • 5 mL balloon: Used primarily in paediatric catheterisation. The smaller balloon reduces the risk of urethral and bladder neck trauma in children.
  • 10 mL balloon: Standard adult volume for most indwelling catheterisation. Provides secure retention without excessive bladder wall pressure.
  • 30 mL balloon: Used in haemostatic applications (post-prostatectomy bleeding control) and situations requiring enhanced catheter retention. The larger balloon exerts greater pressure on the bladder neck and should not be used for routine catheterisation.
  • 50–75 mL balloon: Specialised use in urological surgery for post-operative haemostasis and tamponade. Not appropriate for general ward use.

Always inflate the balloon with sterile water only — never with saline, which can crystallise inside the balloon and impede deflation during catheter removal. The balloon inflation volume is printed on the catheter port; never exceed the stated volume.

Catheter-Associated Urinary Tract Infections (CAUTIs)

CAUTIs are the most common healthcare-associated infection linked to indwelling urinary catheters. Each day a catheter remains in place, the risk of bacteriuria increases by approximately three to seven per cent. For patients catheterised for more than thirty days, bacteriuria is essentially universal. While not all bacteriuria progresses to clinical infection, CAUTIs result in extended hospital stays, additional antibiotic treatment, increased healthcare costs, and in severe cases, urosepsis and mortality.

Effective CAUTI prevention strategies include:

  • Appropriate catheter indication: Ensure every catheter insertion has a documented clinical indication. Avoid catheterisation for convenience or incontinence management when alternatives (external catheters, scheduled toileting, absorbent products) are appropriate.
  • Aseptic insertion technique: Catheter insertion must follow strict aseptic non-touch technique (ANTT) with sterile gloves, antiseptic cleansing of the urethral meatus, sterile lubrication, and a sterile field. This is the single most important modifiable factor in reducing early-onset CAUTIs.
  • Closed drainage systems: Maintain a closed, unobstructed drainage system. Break the circuit only when medically necessary (e.g., catheter change or irrigation). Position the drainage bag below the level of the bladder to prevent urine reflux.
  • Timely removal: Review catheter necessity daily and remove as soon as the clinical indication resolves. Consider nurse-driven removal protocols and catheter reminder systems, which have been shown to significantly reduce catheter days and CAUTI rates.
  • Antimicrobial-impregnated catheters: For patients at high risk of CAUTI or with a history of recurrent catheter-associated infections, silver alloy-coated or antibiotic-impregnated catheters may reduce bacteriuria rates. However, these catheters carry a higher per-unit cost and evidence for their impact on symptomatic UTI rates (as opposed to asymptomatic bacteriuria) is mixed. Use selectively based on patient risk assessment.

International Standards and Regulatory Compliance

ISO 13485 — Medical Device Quality Management

Manufacturers of urinary catheters must operate under an ISO 13485 certified quality management system. This standard governs design control, raw material traceability, cleanroom manufacturing environments, in-process testing, and post-market surveillance. For catheter products, ISO 13485 compliance ensures that every production lot meets consistent specifications for balloon integrity, catheter lumen patency, connector fit, and sterility assurance.

ISO 10993 — Biocompatibility Testing

ISO 10993 requires biocompatibility evaluation of all materials that contact patient tissue. Urinary catheters, which contact mucosal membranes and the bladder lining, must pass cytotoxicity, sensitisation, and intracutaneous irritation tests. Manufacturers should provide biocompatibility test reports for each catheter material (latex, silicone, PVC, hydrogel coating, balloon material, and adhesive components for external catheters).

US FDA Classification

In the United States, urinary catheters are Class II medical devices requiring 510(k) premarket notification. Manufacturers must demonstrate substantial equivalence to a predicate device and comply with applicable FDA guidance documents on catheter design, labelling, and performance testing. Imported catheters must be from FDA-registered establishments with current listing of the catheter product line.

EU MDR and CE Marking

Under the European Union Medical Device Regulation (MDR) 2017/745, urinary catheters are generally classified as Class I or Class IIa devices depending on their duration of use and specific features. Manufacturers must maintain a technical file, conduct a clinical evaluation, and obtain CE marking through a notified body assessment for Class IIa devices. Disposable urinary catheters placed on the EU market must bear the CE mark and be accompanied by instructions for use in the language(s) of the destination country.

Procurement Best Practices

Maintain a Standardised Catheter Formulary

Develop a tiered urinary catheter formulary with clear clinical indications for each tier. Tier one covers standard latex Foley catheters in common sizes (14 Fr, 16 Fr) with 10 mL balloons for short-term general use. Tier two covers silicone Foley catheters for latex-allergic patients and medium-term catheterisation. Tier three covers specialty catheters (three-way irrigation, antimicrobial-coated, suprapubic, large-bore) for specific clinical scenarios. A structured formulary prevents unnecessary variation in catheter selection, consolidates purchasing volume, and enables more competitive pricing from suppliers.

Balance Stock Levels Across Sizes

Stock catheters across the full range of clinical sizes (12 Fr through 22 Fr for adult Foley catheters), but weight inventory toward the most frequently used sizes. In most adult patient populations, 14 Fr and 16 Fr account for approximately sixty to seventy per cent of catheter usage. Smaller sizes (12 Fr) are needed for patients with narrow urethras or urethral strictures, and larger sizes (18–22 Fr) for patients with haematuria or post-operative irrigation needs. Maintain smaller quantities of less-frequently-used sizes and establish rapid reorder protocols for specialty sizes to avoid overstocking slow-moving inventory.

Verify Sterility and Packaging Integrity

All indwelling and intermittent urinary catheters should be supplied sterile, individually packaged in peel-open pouches with clear lot numbering and expiry date. Upon receipt, inspect packaging integrity — torn, crushed, or moisture-compromised packages must be rejected. Verify that sterility claims are supported by validated sterilisation processes (ethylene oxide or gamma irradiation) and that shelf life is consistent with the manufacturer's validated shelf-life studies. Typical shelf life for sterile urinary catheters is three to five years.

Storage and Shelf-Life Management

Disposable urinary catheters should be stored in their original, sealed packaging in a clean, dry, temperature-controlled environment (15°C to 30°C), protected from direct sunlight and excessive humidity. Avoid stacking heavy items on catheter packages, as compression can deform catheter shafts and compromise sterility barriers. Implement FIFO (first-in-first-out) stock rotation and monitor expiry dates proactively. Latex catheters may degrade over extended storage periods; check for discoloration, tackiness, or odour changes before use. Hydrophilic-coated intermittent catheters should be inspected for coating integrity — dried-out or delaminated coatings indicate product degradation and the catheter should not be used.
