Disposable Oropharyngeal and Nasopharyngeal Airways: A Complete Guide for Healthcare Procurement

A comprehensive guide to disposable oropharyngeal (Guedel) and nasopharyngeal (nasal trumpet) airways for hospitals, emergency departments, and medical distributors. Compare airway types, sizing methods, materials, design features, and procurement best practices for basic airway management.

When a patient loses consciousness, the tongue and soft tissues of the oropharynx fall backward against the posterior pharyngeal wall, creating a complete or partial airway obstruction. This is the single most common cause of airway compromise in unconscious patients and the first problem every clinician must address in an emergency. Before any advanced airway device is deployed, before laryngoscopy is performed, and before an endotracheal tube is placed, the first line of defence is a basic airway adjunct — the oropharyngeal airway (OPA, commonly known as a Guedel airway) or the nasopharyngeal airway (NPA, commonly known as a nasal trumpet).

These simple, low-cost devices are among the most frequently used tools in emergency medicine, anaesthesia, prehospital care, and critical care. An oropharyngeal airway is a rigid, curved plastic device that displaces the tongue anteriorly, creating a patent channel from the mouth to the posterior pharynx. A nasopharyngeal airway is a soft, flexible tube inserted through the nostril and advanced through the nasal passage into the posterior pharynx, bypassing upper airway obstruction while the patient maintains spontaneous breathing.

The global market for basic airway adjuncts continues to grow steadily, driven by the expansion of emergency medical services, the increasing availability of basic life support training, rising awareness of airway management in prehospital settings, and ongoing demand from hospitals, ambulances, dental clinics, and first-aid kits worldwide. For procurement teams, maintaining an adequate and well-organised inventory of oropharyngeal and nasopharyngeal airways across all required sizes is a fundamental responsibility — these devices are small, inexpensive, and absolutely essential when seconds count.

Oropharyngeal Airways (Guedel Airways)

Design and Function

An oropharyngeal airway is a rigid, curved plastic device with a flange at the proximal end (resting against the patient's lips or teeth) and a curved body that follows the anatomical contour of the oral cavity. The distal end features a bite block channel that prevents the patient from occluding the airway by biting down, and side channels that allow suction catheters to pass through if needed. The curve of the device is designed to lift the base of the tongue forward and upward, preventing it from falling against the posterior pharyngeal wall.

Two primary design styles are commonly available:

Design Type Characteristics Best For
Guedel design Classic curved body with a central airway channel and lateral bite block wings; wide open design allows maximal airflow and easy suction access; the most widely used OPA design globally General hospital use; emergency departments; ambulance services; basic and advanced life support; the standard choice for most clinical settings
Berman design Two lateral airway channels with a solid central body; more robust construction; slightly less central airflow but greater structural strength; bite block integrated into the lateral channels Situations where enhanced durability is needed; patients with strong bite reflex; settings where lateral suction access is preferred

OPA insertion requires the patient to be unconscious or deeply sedated, as the device stimulates the gag reflex in conscious or semi-conscious patients. The standard insertion technique involves selecting the correct size, opening the airway with a head-tilt chin-lift or jaw thrust, inserting the device upside down (curved toward the palate) until it reaches the soft palate, then rotating it 180 degrees so the curve follows the tongue. An alternative technique uses a tongue depressor to visualise the oropharynx and insert the device right-side-up directly into position.

Sizing Guide for Oropharyngeal Airways

Correct sizing is critical. An OPA that is too small will not effectively lift the tongue and may push it further back, worsening the obstruction. An OPA that is too large can cause trauma to the posterior pharyngeal wall, trigger the gag reflex even in sedated patients, or advance too far into the hypopharynx.

4 100 mm Yellow Adults (small to average)

A quick bedside sizing method for adults: measure the distance from the corner of the mouth to the angle of the jaw or to the tip of the ear lobe. The OPA that most closely matches this measurement is the correct size. For paediatric patients, measure from the corner of the mouth to the angle of the mandible. This visual estimation method is reliable and can be performed in seconds during an emergency.

Procurement tip: For adult emergency carts and ambulance kits, stock size 4 as the default adult size, with sizes 3 and 5 as back-ups. For paediatric areas, maintain sizes 0 through 3. OPA devices are colour-coded by size in most product lines — use the colour coding to speed up identification during emergencies and simplify inventory management.

Nasopharyngeal Airways (Nasal Trumpets)

Design and Function

A nasopharyngeal airway is a soft, flexible tube with a bevelled distal tip and a flared flange at the proximal end. Unlike oropharyngeal airways, NPAs can be used in conscious or semi-conscious patients because they do not stimulate the gag reflex as intensely. They are particularly valuable in patients with trismus (locked jaw), oral trauma or swelling, seizures, or when an oral route is contraindicated.

NPAs are manufactured from soft medical-grade PVC or rubber-like polymers that are flexible enough to conform to the nasal passage anatomy. The distal end features a bevelled cut and may include side eyes (additional openings) to ensure airflow even if the primary bevel contacts the pharyngeal wall. Some designs incorporate a safety flange at the proximal end to prevent the device from being accidentally aspirated into the nasal cavity — a rare but serious complication if a non-flanged NPA is inserted too deeply without securing the external end.

Sizing Guide for Nasopharyngeal Airways

NPA sizing is based on two parameters: the external diameter (measured in French gauge, Fr) and the length (measured in millimetres). Both parameters must be appropriate for the patient.

External Diameter (Fr) Approx. Internal Diameter Length (mm) Patient
22–24 Fr ~3.0–3.3 mm 70–80 mm Infants and young children
26–28 Fr ~3.7–4.0 mm 80–90 mm Older children
30–32 Fr ~4.3–4.7 mm 90–100 mm Small adults; average female
34–36 Fr ~5.0–5.3 mm 100–110 mm Average adult male
38–40 Fr ~5.7–6.0 mm 110–120 mm Large adult male

A quick bedside sizing method: measure the distance from the tip of the patient's nose to the tragus of the ear. The NPA that most closely matches this length is the correct choice. For diameter, select the largest size that fits comfortably into the patient's nostril. The NPA should fit snugly but without resistance — forcing an oversized NPA can cause nasal mucosal trauma and significant epistaxis (nosebleed).

NPAs should be lubricated with a water-based lubricant or lidocaine gel before insertion to reduce friction and patient discomfort. Insertion is typically performed through the more patent nostril. If resistance is encountered, rotate the device gently during insertion or try the other nostril. NPAs are contraindicated in patients with suspected skull base fractures (cribriform plate injury) or severe nasal pathology due to the risk of intracranial insertion.

Material Selection

The material of basic airway adjuncts affects patient comfort, durability, cost, and reusability classification. Modern practice has shifted decisively toward single-use disposable devices for infection control reasons, but understanding material properties remains important for procurement decisions.

Material Device Type Characteristics
Medical-grade PVC (rigid) Oropharyngeal airways Transparent; rigid enough to maintain airway patency; smooth edges; biocompatible; cost-effective; ideal for single-use sterile packaging; the dominant material for disposable OPAs
Medical-grade PVC (soft) Nasopharyngeal airways Flexible; conforms to nasal passage anatomy; soft bevelled tip reduces mucosal trauma; can be manufactured in graduated stiffness; the standard material for disposable NPAs
Latex-free rubber Nasopharyngeal airways (less common) Very soft and flexible; excellent patient comfort; higher cost than PVC; latex allergy concerns limit use in modern procurement; largely replaced by soft PVC formulations

All modern disposable airway adjuncts should be latex-free and DEHP-free. Single-use devices eliminate the need for reprocessing (cleaning, disinfection, or sterilisation), reduce the risk of cross-contamination between patients, and ensure that each device is in optimal condition with no fatigue, deformation, or micro-cracking from repeated use.

Clinical Applications

Emergency Medicine and Prehospital Care

Basic airway adjuncts are the first devices deployed in cardiac arrest, overdose, seizure, trauma, and any scenario where a patient has a depressed level of consciousness. In the ABC (Airway, Breathing, Circulation) approach, an OPA or NPA is the primary intervention after manual airway manoeuvres (head-tilt chin-lift, jaw thrust) prove insufficient. Every ambulance, first-response vehicle, and emergency medical kit should carry a complete range of both device types.

Anaesthesia and Operating Theatre

During induction of general anaesthesia, before the patient is intubated, an OPA is routinely used to maintain airway patency during the brief period of unconsciousness before definitive airway control is established. NPAs are used in dental and oral surgery where an oral device would obstruct the operative field, and in emergence from anaesthesia when the patient is semi-conscious and at risk of airway obstruction but not yet ready for extubation.

Intensive Care and Critical Care

NPAs are frequently used in sedated ICU patients who are breathing spontaneously but have reduced pharyngeal tone. They are less invasive than endotracheal tubes or supraglottic airways and allow the patient to maintain spontaneous breathing while ensuring a patent upper airway. OPAs may be used in deeply sedated or paralysed patients as a temporary measure during procedures or airway device changes.

Basic Life Support and First Aid

OPA devices are a standard component of basic life support (BLS) kits, first-aid stations, and automated external defibrillator (AED) bags. They are among the simplest and most effective tools for maintaining an airway in an unconscious casualty and are taught in standard first-aid and BLS training courses worldwide.

Quality Evaluation Checklist

When assessing oropharyngeal and nasopharyngeal airway suppliers, evaluate product quality using the following criteria:

  • Edge smoothness: Run a gloved finger along the entire length of the device. There should be no moulding flash, sharp edges, or burrs that could cause mucosal trauma during insertion or removal. The bevel on NPAs should be smoothly finished.
  • Structural integrity (OPA): Apply gentle bending force to an oropharyngeal airway. It should be rigid enough to maintain its shape and prevent tongue occlusion, but not so brittle that it fractures under pressure. The bite block should withstand moderate bite force without deformation.
  • Flexibility (NPA): A nasopharyngeal airway should be flexible enough to conform to nasal passage anatomy without kinking. Excessively stiff NPAs increase the risk of mucosal trauma; excessively soft NPAs may collapse under nasal tissue pressure.
  • Flange design: The proximal flange should be wide enough to prevent the device from being fully inserted into the oral or nasal cavity. OPA flanges should rest securely against the patient's lips. NPA flanges should be flat and broad enough to prevent accidental aspiration.
  • Colour coding clarity: Size colour coding should be clear, durable, and resistant to fading. Rapid visual identification of the correct size is essential in emergency situations. Verify that colour coding follows the standard scheme used in your target markets.
  • Individual packaging: Each device should be individually packaged in a sterile peel-open pouch with lot number, expiry date, and size clearly visible. Individually packaged devices are essential for emergency cart organisation and single-use compliance.
  • Latex-free and DEHP-free confirmation: Verify that product labelling and documentation confirm the absence of latex and DEHP (diethylhexyl phthalate), which can cause allergic reactions and toxicological concerns.
Tip for procurement teams: Organise airway adjunct kits by size with clear colour-coded labelling. For emergency departments and ambulance services, pre-assemble airway kits containing a graduated range of both OPA and NPA sizes, lubricating gel, and a bite block. Pre-packed kits save critical seconds during emergency airway management.

Regulatory Standards and Compliance

ISO 13485 — Quality Management

Manufacturers operating under an ISO 13485 certified quality management system demonstrate consistent control over raw material selection, injection moulding or extrusion process parameters, dimensional inspection, finished product testing, batch traceability, and sterility assurance. For basic airway adjuncts, this includes verification of dimensional accuracy (length, diameter, curvature radius), biocompatibility of materials, and sterility of packaging.

ISO 10993 — Biocompatibility

Oropharyngeal and nasopharyngeal airways contact mucosal tissue and should comply with relevant ISO 10993 biocompatibility requirements. Testing typically includes cytotoxicity, sensitisation, and irritation assessments. All patient-contacting materials should be latex-free and DEHP-free.

Sterility Standards

Single-use disposable airway adjuncts are supplied sterile, typically via ethylene oxide (EO) sterilisation or gamma irradiation. Sterile packaging must display the sterilisation method, lot number, expiry date, and sterility integrity indicators. Individual peel-open packaging enables aseptic presentation of the device during emergency use.

CE Marking and FDA Registration

In the European Union, basic airway adjuncts are classified as medical devices under the Medical Device Regulation (MDR) and require CE marking. In the United States, they are generally Class I devices subject to general controls and establishment registration. Procurement teams serving regulated markets should verify the manufacturer's registration status and conformity assessment documentation.

Procurement Best Practices

Stock the Full Size Range

Unlike some medical devices where a limited size range may suffice, airway adjuncts must cover the full spectrum from premature neonates to large adults. Every emergency response point — whether a hospital resuscitation trolley, an ambulance, or a first-aid station — should carry the complete graduated range. Missing sizes create gaps in care that can be life-threatening.

Pre-Assemble Airway Kits

For emergency departments, operating theatres, and ambulance services, consider pre-assembled airway management kits that include a graduated range of OPA and NPA sizes, a lubricant sachet, and a tongue depressor in a single sterile package. Pre-assembled kits reduce set-up time, ensure completeness, and simplify inventory management. They also reduce the risk of missing sizes during an emergency response.

Maintain High Safety Stock

Basic airway adjuncts are low-cost, high-usage devices. Running out of a specific size is unacceptable in an emergency care setting. Maintain a minimum of 8–12 weeks of safety stock, particularly for the most commonly used sizes (adult sizes 3, 4, and 5 for OPAs; 30–36 Fr for NPAs). The low unit cost makes overstocking a minimal financial risk compared to the clinical risk of stockouts.

Standardise Across Your Organisation

Where possible, standardise on one OPA design (Guedel or Berman) and one NPA type across all departments and response vehicles. Standardisation ensures that clinicians can locate and use the correct device without hesitation, regardless of which cart or kit they access. It also simplifies procurement negotiations and reduces the number of SKUs to manage.

Storage and Handling

Store airway adjuncts in their original sterile packaging in a clean, dry environment away from direct sunlight, extreme temperatures, and chemical vapours. Implement FIFO (first in, first out) stock rotation. OPA devices made from rigid PVC are relatively durable, but excessive heat can cause warping or deformation. NPA devices made from soft PVC should not be compressed or stored under heavy items, as permanent deformation could affect their performance. Individually packaged devices should be inspected before use to confirm packaging integrity.

Environmental Considerations

As single-use medical devices made primarily from PVC, disposable airway adjuncts contribute to medical plastic waste. Procurement teams can reduce environmental impact by selecting manufacturers who minimise packaging materials, use recyclable outer packaging, and implement lean manufacturing practices. Some manufacturers offer take-back programmes for non-contaminated outer packaging. While the clinical-contact device itself must be disposed of through clinical waste streams after use, selecting suppliers committed to sustainable manufacturing practices is one way procurement teams can address environmental concerns without compromising patient safety.
