Disposable Wound Dressings: Types, Materials, and Procurement Guide

Disposable wound dressings are essential medical supplies used across every healthcare setting. This guide covers dressing types, material properties, international standards, wound healing phases, and practical procurement strategies for hospitals, clinics, and medical supply distributors.

Disposable wound dressings represent one of the largest categories of medical consumables used in hospitals, emergency departments, primary care clinics, and home healthcare settings worldwide. Every day, millions of wounds — from minor abrasions and surgical incisions to complex chronic ulcers and traumatic injuries — require appropriate dressing materials to protect the wound bed, manage exudate, prevent infection, and promote optimal healing. The global wound care dressing market is valued at over twenty-two billion US dollars and continues to grow steadily, driven by an ageing population, increasing prevalence of chronic wounds such as diabetic foot ulcers and pressure injuries, and rising surgical volumes worldwide.

For healthcare procurement professionals, understanding the full range of disposable wound dressing options — from simple gauze and adhesive bandages to advanced hydrocolloid and foam dressings — is essential for maintaining clinical quality while managing supply chain costs. Selecting the wrong dressing for a given wound type can delay healing, increase infection risk, cause unnecessary pain during dressing changes, and ultimately raise total treatment costs. This guide provides a comprehensive overview of disposable wound dressing types, their material properties, relevant international standards, and procurement best practices.

The Science of Wound Healing and Dressing Selection

Effective wound dressing selection begins with understanding how wounds heal. Wound healing progresses through four overlapping phases: haemostasis (blood clotting), inflammation (immune response and debris clearance), proliferation (new tissue formation and wound contraction), and remodelling (scar maturation and tissue strengthening). The ideal dressing supports each phase of this process by maintaining an appropriate wound environment.

Research dating back to the pioneering work on moist wound healing demonstrates that wounds heal significantly faster in a moist environment compared to dry conditions. A moist wound bed facilitates cell migration, reduces scab formation that impedes epithelialisation, and supports the activity of growth factors and white blood cells. Modern advanced wound dressings are engineered to maintain this optimal moist environment while managing excess exudate that can macerate surrounding healthy skin. Dry gauze dressings, by contrast, can adhere to the wound bed and cause trauma and pain during removal — a phenomenon known as "re-wounding" that disrupts newly formed tissue.

Classification of Disposable Wound Dressings

Traditional Dressings

Traditional wound dressings have been used in healthcare for decades and remain widely applied for basic wound coverage, absorption, and protection.

  • Gauze dressings (woven and non-woven): Made from cotton, polyester, or blended fibres, gauze dressings are versatile, absorbent, and economical. Woven gauze offers higher absorbency and is suitable for packing deep wounds, while non-woven gauze is softer and less likely to leave lint residue. Gauze is used as a primary dressing for low-exudate wounds and as a secondary dressing to secure other primary dressings. Sterile gauze swabs are among the most frequently used items in surgical and emergency departments.
  • Abdominal pads (ABD pads): Large, highly absorbent dressings used for post-surgical wound coverage, heavy exudate management, and trauma wound dressing. Typically constructed with a soft non-woven facing and an absorbent cellulose core, ABD pads are available in multiple sizes with sewn edges to prevent fibre shedding.
  • Adhesive bandages (first aid plasters): Small, self-adhesive dressings combining an absorbent pad with a flexible adhesive backing. Used for minor cuts, abrasions, and blisters in both clinical and consumer settings. Available in various shapes, sizes, and materials including fabric, plastic, and foam backings. Many are offered in assorted packs to accommodate different wound locations and sizes.
  • Paraffin gauze (tulle gras): Impregnated with soft paraffin or other non-adherent coatings to prevent sticking to the wound bed. Ideal for burns, skin grafts, donor sites, and other wounds where dressing removal trauma must be minimised. The non-adherent layer allows exudate to pass through to a secondary absorbent dressing while protecting the wound surface.

Advanced Dressings

Advanced wound dressings incorporate engineered materials designed to actively support the wound healing environment.

  • Hydrocolloid dressings: Composed of gel-forming agents such as carboxymethylcellulose, gelatin, and pectin bonded to a waterproof polyurethane or foam backing. Upon contact with wound exudate, the hydrocolloid layer absorbs fluid and forms a gel, maintaining a moist wound environment. Hydrocolloid dressings are self-adhesive, waterproof, and typically remain in place for three to seven days. They are particularly effective for low to moderate exudate wounds, including pressure ulcers (Stage I–II), minor burns, and donor sites.
  • Foam dressings: Made from polyurethane foam, these dressings offer high absorbency, cushioning, and thermal insulation. Foam dressings manage moderate to heavy exudate effectively, drawing fluid away from the wound bed into the foam structure where it is retained. They are available in both adhesive and non-adhesive versions and are suitable for pressure ulcers, surgical wounds, leg ulcers, and traumatic wounds. Their cushioning property provides additional protection for bony prominences and areas prone to friction.
  • Hydrogel dressings: Water-based or glycerine-based gel dressings that donate moisture to dry or necrotic wounds, facilitating autolytic debridement. Hydrogel dressings are ideal for dry wounds, eschar, and sloughy wounds that require rehydration. They are available as amorphous gels in tubes or sachets, and as impregnated sheet dressings for easier application. Hydrogels provide a cooling effect that can reduce pain in burn wounds.
  • Alginate dressings: Derived from brown seaweed (alginic acid), alginate dressings are highly absorbent, capable of absorbing up to twenty times their weight in exudate. Upon contact with wound fluid, calcium ions in the alginate exchange with sodium ions in the exudate, forming a soft gel that maintains a moist environment while locking away excess fluid. Alginate dressings are particularly suitable for heavily exuding wounds, cavity wounds, and bleeding wounds (alginate has mild haemostatic properties). They are available as ropes for packing and as flat sheets for surface wounds.
  • Film dressings (transparent semi-permeable membranes): Thin, transparent polyurethane films with adhesive borders that allow oxygen and water vapour to pass through while blocking bacteria and liquids. Film dressings are used as secondary dressings, for securing intravenous catheters, and for superficial wounds with minimal exudate. Their transparency allows visual wound monitoring without removal.

Dressing Selection by Wound Type

Choosing the right dressing depends on wound type, exudate level, wound depth, infection status, and the phase of healing.

Wound Type Exudate Level Recommended Dressing
Surgical incision Low Film dressing, sterile gauze, adhesive bandage
Pressure ulcer (Stage I–II) Low to moderate Hydrocolloid, thin foam
Pressure ulcer (Stage III–IV) Moderate to heavy Foam, alginate, hydrocolloid foam combination
Diabetic foot ulcer Variable Hydrocolloid, foam, alginate (with antimicrobial if infected)
Venous leg ulcer Moderate to heavy Foam, alginate, multi-layer compression systems
Burns (partial thickness) Moderate Hydrogel, hydrocolloid, silicone contact layer
Donor sites Low to moderate Film, hydrocolloid, silicone mesh
Cavity / tunneling wounds Moderate to heavy Alginate rope, hydrogel rope, foam packing
Abrasions and lacerations Low Adhesive bandage, paraffin gauze, film dressing

Antimicrobial Dressings

For infected or critically colonised wounds, antimicrobial dressings provide an additional line of defence by reducing the bioburden within the wound bed. Common antimicrobial agents incorporated into wound dressings include:

  • Silver (ionic and nanocrystalline): Silver has broad-spectrum antimicrobial activity against bacteria, fungi, and some viruses. Silver-impregnated dressings release silver ions into the wound environment, disrupting microbial cell membranes and metabolic processes. Silver dressings are indicated for infected wounds, wounds at high risk of infection, and chronic wounds showing signs of critical colonisation.
  • Iodine (cadexomer iodine, povidone-iodine): Iodine-based dressings provide sustained release of free iodine, which has potent antimicrobial and debriding properties. Cadexomer iodine dressings absorb exudate while releasing iodine, making them particularly effective for sloughy and infected wounds.
  • Polyhexamethylene biguanide (PHMB): A synthetic antimicrobial agent effective against a broad range of wound pathogens including biofilm-forming bacteria. PHMB-impregnated dressings are well tolerated and suitable for extended use in chronic wound management.
  • Medical honey (medicinal-grade Manuka honey): Medical-grade honey dressings provide antimicrobial activity through osmotic action, low pH, and hydrogen peroxide release. Honey also supports autolytic debridement and has anti-inflammatory properties. It is suitable for infected wounds, malodorous wounds, and wounds with slough or necrotic tissue.
Clinical note: Antimicrobial dressings should be used for a defined period (typically two to four weeks) with regular reassessment. If signs of infection have resolved, transition to a non-antimicrobial dressing appropriate for the wound phase. Prolonged use of antimicrobial dressings without indication is not recommended.

International Standards and Regulatory Compliance

Disposable wound dressings are regulated medical devices subject to quality, safety, and performance standards across major markets.

ISO 13485 — Medical Device Quality Management

Manufacturers of disposable wound dressings should operate under an ISO 13485 certified quality management system. This standard ensures that manufacturing processes, raw material sourcing, cleanroom environments, and product testing meet internationally recognised requirements for medical device safety and consistency.

ISO 10993 — Biological Evaluation of Medical Devices

ISO 10993 covers the biological evaluation of medical devices, including cytotoxicity, sensitisation, and irritation testing. Wound dressings that come into direct contact with wound beds must pass biocompatibility tests to ensure they do not cause adverse tissue reactions. Procurement teams should verify that suppliers have conducted ISO 10993 testing for all dressing materials and adhesive components.

EN 13726 — Test Methods for Primary Wound Dressings

EN 13726 is the European standard specifying test methods for primary wound dressings. It addresses key performance characteristics including moisture vapour transmission rate, fluid handling capacity, absorbency, conformability, and bacterial barrier properties. Dressings tested to this standard provide quantifiable performance data that supports evidence-based product selection.

US FDA Classification

In the United States, wound dressings are generally classified as Class I or Class II medical devices depending on their composition and intended use. Simple gauze and adhesive bandages are typically Class I (exempt from premarket notification), while advanced dressings with antimicrobial agents or novel materials may require Class II 510(k) clearance. Imported wound dressings must comply with FDA establishment registration and listing requirements.

Procurement Best Practices

Build a Tiered Formulary

Develop a tiered wound dressing formulary that aligns dressing types with wound categories. Tier one covers basic dressings (gauze, adhesive bandages, ABD pads) for minor wounds and secondary dressing use. Tier two covers intermediate dressings (film, paraffin gauze, simple foam) for moderate wounds. Tier three covers advanced dressings (hydrocolloid, alginate, hydrogel, antimicrobial) for complex and chronic wounds. A structured formulary ensures that clinicians select the most cost-effective dressing for each wound type, reserving premium advanced dressings for wounds where their benefits are clinically justified.

Standardise Sizes and Configurations

Within each dressing category, standardise the sizes and configurations stocked. For example, maintaining foam dressings in 10 × 10 cm, 15 × 15 cm, and 20 × 20 cm sizes covers the majority of clinical use cases. Avoid stocking excessive size variations that fragment purchasing volume and complicate inventory management. Similarly, choose between adhesive and non-adhesive configurations based on the predominant use patterns in your facility — adhesive dressings simplify application but may cause skin trauma in patients with fragile skin, while non-adhesive dressings require secondary fixation but are more versatile.

Assess Supplier Quality and Consistency

Wound dressing quality directly affects patient outcomes. When evaluating suppliers, consider the following factors: ISO 13485 certification and audit history; consistency of product specifications across production batches; availability of CE marking documentation and Declaration of Conformity; biocompatibility test reports (ISO 10993); packaging integrity and sterility assurance validation; and track record of on-time delivery and responsive customer support. Request clinical samples for evaluation before committing to volume orders, and conduct wear trials in your facility to assess real-world performance including ease of application, patient comfort, exudate management, and adhesive skin tolerance.

Consider Total Cost of Care

The per-unit price of a wound dressing is only one component of the total cost of wound management. Advanced dressings may have a higher per-unit cost but can reduce dressing change frequency (from daily to every three to seven days), lower nursing time, decrease infection rates, and accelerate wound closure — all of which reduce total treatment cost. When evaluating dressing options, consider the cost per dressing change episode rather than the cost per unit, factoring in nursing time, ancillary supplies, and the expected number of dressing changes required for wound closure.

Storage and Handling Guidelines

Disposable wound dressings should be stored in their original, unopened packaging in a clean, dry environment at controlled room temperature (15°C to 30°C). Protect dressings from direct sunlight, excessive humidity, and temperature extremes. Hydrogel dressings are particularly sensitive to freezing, which can alter their gel structure and reduce performance. Hydrocolloid dressings may lose adhesive properties if exposed to high humidity or temperatures above 30°C. Ensure warehouse shelving is clean and dry, and implement FIFO (first-in-first-out) stock rotation. Check packaging integrity on receipt — torn, crushed, or opened packages compromise sterility and should be rejected. Typical shelf life for sterile wound dressings is three to five years from manufacture, depending on the product and packaging configuration.
