Disposable Endotracheal Tubes: A Complete Guide for Healthcare Procurement

A comprehensive guide to disposable endotracheal tubes for hospitals, clinics, and medical distributors. Compare cuffed and uncuffed designs, understand internal diameter sizing, evaluate cuff technologies and tube materials, and learn procurement best practices for secure airway management.

Securing a definitive airway is one of the most fundamental and time-critical procedures in modern medicine. Whether in an operating theatre during general anaesthesia, in an emergency department following traumatic injury, or in an intensive care unit for prolonged mechanical ventilation, the endotracheal tube (ETT) serves as the conduit that connects a patient's airway to the outside world — enabling oxygen delivery, ventilation support, airway protection from aspiration, and the administration of inhaled medications. The choice of endotracheal tube directly impacts intubation success rates, patient safety, ventilation effectiveness, and the risk of airway-related complications.

Historically, endotracheal tubes were made from red rubber and required meticulous cleaning, sterilisation, and inspection between each use. The transition to single-use disposable endotracheal tubes — constructed from advanced medical-grade polymers with integrated cuffs, standardized connectors, and clear depth markings — has been one of the most significant improvements in airway management safety. Disposable ETTs eliminate cross-contamination risk between patients, guarantee consistent dimensional accuracy, and offer a wide range of specialised designs tailored to specific clinical scenarios, from paediatric anaesthesia to difficult airway management.

The global endotracheal tube market continues to grow steadily, driven by increasing surgical volumes worldwide, the expansion of emergency medical services, rising demand for intensive care capacity, and ongoing technological innovation in cuff design and subglottic suction capability. For hospitals, anaesthesia departments, emergency medicine teams, and medical supply distributors, maintaining a well-managed inventory of endotracheal tubes across the full range of sizes and types is a critical procurement responsibility.

Types of Disposable Endotracheal Tubes

Endotracheal tubes are classified by several design attributes: the presence or absence of a cuff, the intended route of insertion, the curvature of the tube body, and specialised features for specific clinical applications. Understanding these categories is essential for building a comprehensive tube inventory.

Cuffed vs. Uncuffed Tubes

The most fundamental distinction in endotracheal tube design is whether the tube incorporates an inflatable cuff near the distal (patient) end. The cuff serves two critical functions: it creates a seal against the tracheal wall to enable positive pressure ventilation, and it acts as a barrier to prevent aspiration of secretions, gastric contents, and blood into the lower airways.

Cuffed endotracheal tubes are the standard choice for adult and adolescent patients undergoing general anaesthesia or requiring mechanical ventilation in intensive care. The cuff is inflated via a pilot balloon and inflation line after the tube is positioned in the trachea. Modern cuffed ETTs feature high-volume, low-pressure (HVLP) cuffs that distribute inflation pressure over a larger tracheal surface area, reducing the risk of tracheal mucosal ischaemia and subsequent tracheal stenosis — a serious long-term complication of prolonged intubation.

Uncuffed endotracheal tubes are primarily used in paediatric patients, particularly infants and young children, where the naturally narrow subglottic region provides a functional seal without the need for an inflatable cuff. Uncuffed tubes reduce the risk of subglottic stenosis in this vulnerable population. However, the trend in paediatric anaesthesia has shifted in recent years, with many centres now preferring cuffed tubes even for younger children, as modern microcuff designs offer reliable sealing with minimal mucosal pressure. The choice between cuffed and uncuffed in paediatric practice often depends on institutional protocol, clinician preference, and the specific clinical scenario.

Oral vs. Nasal Tubes

Oral endotracheal tubes are the most common type, inserted through the mouth into the trachea. They are the standard route for rapid sequence intubation, general anaesthesia, and emergency airway management. Oral tubes are available in the widest range of sizes and configurations.

Nasal endotracheal tubes are designed for insertion through the nostril, through the nasopharynx, and into the trachea. They are longer and have a more gradual curvature than oral tubes. Nasal intubation is preferred in certain oral and maxillofacial surgical procedures where oral tube placement would obstruct the surgical field, and in situations where prolonged intubation is anticipated and patient comfort is a consideration. Nasal tubes should be used with caution in patients with skull base fractures or significant coagulopathy due to the risk of epistaxis and intracranial passage.

Preformed (RAE) Tubes

Ring-Adair-Elwyn (RAE) tubes are preformed endotracheal tubes with a fixed, predetermined curvature designed to route the tube away from the surgical field in specific procedures. Oral RAE tubes have a preformed bend that directs the tube downward toward the chin, commonly used in dental, oral, and maxillofacial surgery. Nasal RAE tubes route the tube upward along the bridge of the nose, used in cranial, ophthalmic, and certain ENT procedures. The preformed shape reduces the need for additional securing devices and keeps the breathing circuit clear of the operative field.

Reinforced (Armoured / Flexible) Tubes

Reinforced endotracheal tubes contain an embedded spiral wire (typically stainless steel or nylon) within the tube wall, making the tube highly resistant to kinking, bending, and compression. They are essential in procedures where the tube may be subject to positional stress, such as head and neck surgery, prone-position surgery, neurosurgery with head fixation, and paediatric airway management where smaller-diameter tubes are more prone to occlusion. The flexible construction allows the tube to bend without collapsing the airway lumen, though it requires a stylet for initial intubation as the tube cannot hold a shape on its own.

Internal Diameter Sizing

Endotracheal tube sizes are specified by internal diameter (ID) in millimetres. Selecting the correct size is critical: a tube that is too small increases airway resistance, makes suctioning difficult, limits the ability to pass a bronchoscope through the tube, and may result in inadequate ventilation. A tube that is too large can cause laryngeal trauma, vocal cord injury, post-intubation sore throat, and in severe cases, airway oedema requiring re-intubation with a smaller tube.

A widely used formula for estimating paediatric ETT size (uncuffed) is: ID (mm) = (Age in years / 4) + 4. For cuffed paediatric tubes, the formula adjusts to: ID (mm) = (Age in years / 4) + 3.5. These formulas provide a starting point; clinicians should always have tubes one size above and below the estimated size available at intubation.

Procurement tip: For adult general-purpose inventory, stock 7.0 mm, 7.5 mm, and 8.0 mm cuffed oral tubes as your core range. For paediatric wards, maintain a graduated set from 3.0 mm to 6.0 mm in 0.5 mm increments. Always stock at least one size above and below the estimated size for every anticipated patient.

Cuff Technology and Design

The cuff is arguably the most technically significant component of a cuffed endotracheal tube. Cuff design directly affects the quality of the airway seal, the risk of ventilator-associated pneumonia (VAP), and the potential for tracheal mucosal injury during prolonged intubation.

Cuff Type Characteristics Best For
High-volume, low-pressure (HVLP) Larger cuff surface area; lower tracheal wall pressure (typically < 30 cmH₂O when properly inflated); reduces risk of tracheal mucosal ischaemia and post-extubation stenosis Standard adult intubation; ICU mechanical ventilation; prolonged intubation scenarios; the most widely used cuff type
Low-volume, high-pressure Smaller cuff; higher tracheal wall pressure; provides a tight seal with less cuff volume but carries higher risk of mucosal damage Largely obsolete in modern practice; occasionally found in older product lines; generally not recommended for routine use
Tapered cuff (conical) Tapered shape creates a more uniform seal against the tracheal wall; reduces the formation of microchannels through which secretions can pass below the cuff; may reduce VAP risk ICU patients at high risk of VAP; prolonged mechanical ventilation; patients with high gastric aspiration risk
Ultra-thin polyurethane cuff Material thickness of 7–10 micrometres (vs. 15–30 µm for standard PVC cuffs); conforms more closely to tracheal anatomy; fewer microchannels; improved seal at lower cuff pressures Premium product lines; VAP prevention protocols; extended-duration intubation; facilities prioritising patient safety and outcomes

Cuff pressure management is a critical clinical practice consideration. Regardless of cuff type, the cuff should be inflated to the minimum pressure that provides an adequate seal — typically 20–30 cmH₂O. Overinflation causes tracheal mucosal ischaemia, while underinflation permits microaspiration of secretions. Continuous or intermittent cuff pressure monitoring is recommended for all intubated patients, particularly in intensive care settings.

Material Selection

The material of the endotracheal tube body affects its flexibility, kink resistance, biocompatibility, radiopacity, and cost. Most disposable ETTs are manufactured from one of the following materials:

Material Key Properties Best For
Polyvinyl Chloride (PVC) Clear transparency; cost-effective; good flexibility; thermosensitive (softens at body temperature); compatible with radiopaque additives; the dominant material for disposable ETTs General-purpose oral and nasal intubation; standard anaesthesia and emergency use; the widest product availability
Silicone Excellent biocompatibility; minimal tissue reactivity; maintains consistent flexibility across temperature ranges; higher cost than PVC Patients with known PVC sensitivity; premium product lines; applications requiring minimal tissue reaction during prolonged intubation
PVC with wire reinforcement Standard PVC body with embedded stainless steel or nylon spiral wire; highly kink-resistant; maintains airway patency under positional stress; requires stylet for intubation Head and neck surgery; prone-position surgery; neurosurgery; paediatric airway management; situations where tube kinking is a risk

All modern disposable endotracheal tubes should be latex-free and DEHP-free to minimise allergenic and toxicological risk. Radiopaque lines embedded within the tube wall are a standard quality feature, enabling tube position verification on chest X-ray — an essential practice for confirming correct placement and detecting migration.

Key Design Features to Evaluate

Murphy Eye

The Murphy eye is a secondary opening on the side of the tube opposite the bevel, named after the American physician George Murphy. It serves as a safety vent: if the primary bevel becomes occluded against the tracheal wall or by secretions, the Murphy eye provides an alternative pathway for gas exchange. All quality endotracheal tubes should include a Murphy eye. The size and shape of the Murphy eye should allow adequate gas flow without compromising the structural integrity of the tube wall.

Bevel Design

The distal tip of the endotracheal tube features an angled cut (bevel) that facilitates passage through the vocal cords during intubation. Standard bevels are suitable for most intubations. Some tubes feature specialised bevel designs — such as the Parker Flex-Tip, which has a tapered, flexible distal tip designed to reduce the likelihood of catching on the vocal cords or arytenoid cartilages during intubation, potentially improving first-pass success rates and reducing laryngeal trauma.

Depth Markings

Quality endotracheal tubes include printed depth markings at regular intervals (typically every 5 mm or 1 cm) along the tube length. These markings enable the clinician to verify and document the insertion depth at the teeth or lips, reducing the risk of endobronchial intubation (tube advanced too far into one main bronchus) or accidental extubation (tube too shallow). Markings should be clear, durable, and resistant to rubbing off during handling or when exposed to secretions.

15 mm Standard Connector

The proximal end of every endotracheal tube should feature a standard 15 mm conical connector (ISO 5356), compatible with standard breathing circuits, bag-valve-mask devices, and heat and moisture exchangers (HMEs). The connector should engage securely without excessive force and should not detach during patient handling or transport.

Pilot Balloon and Inflation Valve

Cuffed ETTs include a pilot balloon that inflates when the cuff is inflated, providing a visual indication of cuff inflation status. The inflation valve (typically a one-way valve) should maintain cuff pressure reliably over extended periods without leaking. A deflating or non-inflating pilot balloon indicates a cuff leak, which compromises the airway seal and may require tube replacement.

Specialised Endotracheal Tube Types

Subglottic Suction Endotracheal Tubes

These specialised cuffed ETTs include an additional suction lumen that opens above the cuff, in the subglottic space. This lumen allows continuous or intermittent suction of secretions that accumulate above the cuff — a major source of microaspiration and ventilator-associated pneumonia. Subglottic suction ETTs are recommended for patients expected to require mechanical ventilation for more than 48–72 hours and are considered a best-practice intervention in VAP prevention bundles.

Double-Lumen Endotracheal Tubes

Double-lumen ETTs feature two separate lumens, each with its own cuff, enabling independent ventilation of each lung. They are essential in thoracic surgery where single-lung ventilation is required to collapse one lung while ventilating the other. These are specialised tubes used primarily by thoracic anaesthesiologists and are not part of general hospital inventory.

Laryngectomy Tubes

Laryngectomy tubes are designed for patients who have undergone total laryngectomy, where the trachea is brought directly to the neck surface as a stoma. These shorter tubes are inserted directly into the tracheal stoma and do not pass through the upper airway. They are available in cuffed and uncuffed configurations and in fenestrated and non-fenestrated designs.

Quality Evaluation Checklist

When assessing endotracheal tube suppliers, evaluate product quality using the following criteria:

  • Cuff integrity: Inflate the cuff and observe for leaks over 5–10 minutes. The cuff should hold pressure without noticeable deflation. Check for symmetrical inflation without asymmetrical bulging that could indicate manufacturing defects.
  • Bevel smoothness: Inspect the bevel edge for sharpness, burrs, or irregular moulding lines. A rough bevel can cause vocal cord trauma during intubation. The Murphy eye edges should also be smooth and well-formed.
  • Tube flexibility vs. pushability: The tube should be flexible enough to navigate the airway curvature without causing trauma, but rigid enough to transmit force from the proximal end to the tip during intubation. Reinforced tubes should resist kinking completely even when sharply bent.
  • Connector security: Attach a standard breathing circuit to the 15 mm connector. It should engage securely without excessive force and should not detach when moderate traction is applied.
  • Radiopaque line visibility: Hold the tube against a light source or X-ray viewer. The radiopaque line should be clearly visible along the entire tube length, enabling accurate position verification on chest radiographs.
  • Depth marking clarity and durability: Markings should be clearly legible and should not rub off when the tube is handled firmly with gloved hands. Faded or illegible markings defeat their clinical purpose.
  • Pilot balloon inflation valve: Inflate and deflate the cuff several times. The valve should allow easy inflation, maintain pressure reliably, and allow controlled deflation when the valve is depressed.
  • Lot number and expiry date: Every individually packaged ETT should display the lot number, manufacturing date, expiry date, and sterilisation method clearly on the packaging. Traceability is essential for quality management and recall procedures.
Tip for procurement teams: Include endotracheal tube evaluation in your difficult airway cart standardisation process. Consult with anaesthesia and emergency medicine teams to identify preferred tube features (bevel type, cuff design, material) and ensure consistency across emergency, operating theatre, and ICU tube stocks.

Regulatory Standards and Compliance

ISO 13485 — Quality Management

Manufacturers operating under an ISO 13485 certified quality management system demonstrate consistent control over raw material qualification, extrusion and moulding process parameters, in-process dimensional inspection (internal diameter accuracy, wall thickness, cuff volume), finished product testing, batch traceability, and corrective action procedures. For endotracheal tubes, ISO 13485 coverage extends to dimensional tolerances, connector compatibility testing, cuff leak testing, radiopacity verification, and sterility assurance processes.

ISO 10993 — Biocompatibility

Endotracheal tubes contact mucosal tissue and the respiratory tract during use and should comply with relevant ISO 10993 biocompatibility requirements. Testing typically includes cytotoxicity, sensitisation, irritation, and for prolonged-use tubes, systemic toxicity assessments. All patient-contacting materials should be latex-free and DEHP-free.

Sterility Standards

Disposable endotracheal tubes are supplied sterile, typically via ethylene oxide (EO) sterilisation. Sterile packaging must display the sterilisation method, lot number, expiry date, and sterility integrity indicators. Packaging should protect the tube from contamination during storage and handling until the point of use. Individual peel-open packaging is the standard configuration, enabling aseptic presentation of the tube during intubation.

ISO 7376 and ISO 5356 — Connector Standards

ISO 5356 specifies the dimensions and requirements for 15 mm conical connectors used on endotracheal tubes, ensuring compatibility with standard breathing circuits and ventilation equipment. Compliance with this standard is essential for interoperability. ISO 7376 relates to laryngoscopes used in conjunction with ETTs, and while not directly applicable to tube manufacturing, understanding the relationship between tube and laryngoscope specifications is relevant for procurement teams.

CE Marking and FDA Registration

In the European Union, endotracheal tubes are classified as medical devices under the Medical Device Regulation (MDR) and require CE marking with appropriate conformity assessment. In the United States, ETTs are generally Class II devices requiring 510(k) premarket notification and establishment registration. Procurement teams serving regulated markets should verify the manufacturer's registration status and conformity assessment documentation.

Procurement Best Practices

Maintain a Tiered Size Inventory

Every intubation cart and anaesthesia machine should have access to the estimated correct size plus one size above and below. For adult general-purpose use, this means stocking 7.0 mm, 7.5 mm, and 8.0 mm tubes as core sizes, with 6.5 mm and 8.5 mm as secondary sizes. For paediatric areas, maintain a graduated range from the smallest anticipated size (typically 3.0 mm for neonatal) to the largest paediatric size (typically 6.0 mm) in 0.5 mm increments.

Include Specialised Tubes in the Inventory

While standard cuffed oral ETTs form the bulk of inventory, ensure that reinforced tubes (for head/neck and prone surgery), nasal tubes (for ENT and oral surgery), RAE tubes (for dental and cranial surgery), and subglottic suction tubes (for ICU VAP prevention) are available. These specialised tubes are used less frequently but are critical when needed, and supply interruptions can delay or compromise surgical procedures.

Standardise Across Departments

Where possible, standardise on one or two trusted ETT brands across the emergency department, operating theatres, and intensive care unit. Standardisation reduces the cognitive load on clinicians during emergencies (when they need to know exactly how a product behaves without re-learning it), simplifies staff training, and improves volume-based procurement pricing. Work with anaesthesia, emergency medicine, and critical care leadership to agree on a standard range before committing to volume orders.

Maintain Safety Stock and Supplier Redundancy

Endotracheal tubes are essential airway management devices. Supply interruptions directly impact patient safety and surgical scheduling. Maintain a minimum of 4–6 weeks of safety stock for core sizes and establish relationships with at least two qualified suppliers. For international procurement, account for shipping lead times and maintain higher safety stock levels to buffer against logistics disruptions.

Storage and Handling

Store endotracheal tubes in their original sterile packaging in a clean, dry environment away from direct sunlight, extreme temperatures, and chemical vapours. Implement FIFO (first in, first out) stock rotation to ensure older stock is consumed before its expiry date. Avoid stacking heavy items on ETT packaging, as compression can deform the tube or damage the cuff. For temperature-sensitive PVC tubes, storage temperature should be within the manufacturer's specified range to maintain the tube's intended flexibility characteristics.

Environmental Considerations

As single-use medical devices, disposable endotracheal tubes generate plastic waste. The majority of ETTs are manufactured from PVC, which presents recycling challenges due to biohazardous contamination after use. Procurement teams can reduce environmental impact by selecting manufacturers who minimise packaging materials, use recyclable outer packaging, and implement lean manufacturing practices. The industry is exploring alternative polymer formulations for non-clinical-contact packaging components, though the clinical-contact tube body itself will continue to require single-use disposal through clinical waste streams due to infection control requirements.
