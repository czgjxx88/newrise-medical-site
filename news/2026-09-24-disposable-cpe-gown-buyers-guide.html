CPE Gowns vs PP vs SMS: Choosing the Right Disposable Isolation Gown

How procurement teams and infection control officers can select the optimal isolation gown material for each clinical scenario — a detailed comparison of CPE, PP, SMS, and PP+PE materials with barrier performance, comfort, and cost analysis.

Disposable isolation gowns are one of the most essential pieces of personal protective equipment in any healthcare setting. They protect healthcare workers from exposure to blood, body fluids, and infectious materials, while simultaneously preventing the transfer of pathogens from staff to patients — a critical concern for immunocompromised individuals. But "disposable gown" is not a single product. The material it is made from fundamentally determines its protective capabilities, wearer comfort, durability, and cost. Choosing the wrong gown for a given application means either overspending on protection you do not need or, worse, underprotecting staff in high-risk scenarios.

This article compares the four most commonly specified materials for disposable isolation gowns — CPE (Chlorinated Polyethylene), PP (Polypropylene nonwoven), SMS (Spunbond-Meltblown-Spunbond), and PP+PE (Polypropylene laminated with polyethylene) — and provides practical guidance for procurement teams looking to match gown specification to clinical need.

CPE Gowns — The Waterproof Workhorse

CPE (Chlorinated Polyethylene) gowns are made from a chlorinated thermoplastic material that offers excellent liquid barrier performance at a low cost. CPE is produced by chlorinating high-density polyethylene resin, which improves its flexibility, tear resistance, and chemical resistance compared to standard PE film. The result is a gown material that is fully waterproof, durable enough for demanding clinical tasks, and comfortable to wear for extended periods.

CPE gowns typically feature knitted cuffs, a tie-back or snap closure, and an embossed diamond or crosshatch texture on the surface. This texture is not decorative — it increases the surface area for improved liquid repellency and gives the gown a degree of structural rigidity that helps prevent the material from collapsing onto the wearer's arms during movement.

Key strengths of CPE gowns:

  • Complete liquid impermeability. CPE is a solid thermoplastic film, not a woven fabric. Liquids cannot penetrate the material itself — only seams or openings can allow liquid through. This makes CPE gowns suitable for procedures involving significant fluid exposure, including surgery preparation, wound dressing changes, and obstetric procedures.
  • High tear and puncture resistance. Compared to PP nonwoven gowns, CPE gowns are significantly more resistant to tearing during active patient care. This matters in emergency department settings, where staff are moving quickly and gowns are more likely to snag on equipment or bed rails.
  • Chemical resistance. CPE resists a wider range of chemicals than PP, including certain solvents and disinfectants. For laboratory and pharmacy staff handling hazardous medications, this additional resistance is a meaningful safety benefit.
  • Cost-effectiveness. CPE gowns are among the lowest-cost fully waterproof gown options. Their combination of protection and price makes them the default choice for many hospitals in high-volume use scenarios.

The primary limitation of CPE gowns is breathability — the material does not allow air or moisture vapour to pass through, which can cause warmth and perspiration during extended wear in warm environments. For short-to-medium duration tasks, this is rarely an issue, but for staff working long shifts in non-air-conditioned settings, it can affect comfort and compliance.

PP Nonwoven Gowns — The Lightweight Option

PP (Polypropylene) nonwoven gowns are made from spunbonded polypropylene fibres that are bonded together thermally or chemically to form a fabric-like material. They are the lightest and most breathable of the four gown types discussed here, and also the most economical.

However, PP nonwoven material is not fully waterproof. Its barrier performance depends on the fabric weight (grammage): lighter PP gowns (around 20–25 g/m²) offer only splash resistance, suitable for general nursing care and short patient interactions. Heavier PP gowns (35–45 g/m²) provide improved barrier performance but still cannot match the complete impermeability of CPE or PP+PE.

PP gowns are best suited for:

  • General nursing rounds and patient visits
  • Visitors and ancillary staff in controlled environments
  • Situations where breathability and wearer comfort are priorities
  • Food processing and cleanroom environments where liquid exposure is minimal

For infection control officers, PP gowns represent a trade-off: superior comfort and lower cost, but reduced protection against liquid-borne pathogens. They are not recommended for procedures involving blood, body fluids, or infectious materials.

SMS Gowns — The Performance Layer

SMS (Spunbond-Meltblown-Spunbond) is a three-layer composite nonwoven fabric. The outer and inner spunbond layers provide strength and softness, while the middle meltblown layer — made from ultrafine fibres — acts as a high-efficiency filtration and barrier layer. The meltblown layer is the same technology used in the critical filtration layer of medical face masks, and it is exceptionally effective at blocking microorganisms and fine particles.

SMS gowns offer a compelling balance:

  • Microbial barrier performance. The meltblown middle layer provides excellent bacterial filtration efficiency (BFE), typically exceeding 95% for standard-weight SMS fabrics. This makes SMS gowns suitable for surgical and procedural environments where airborne and droplet-borne pathogens are a concern.
  • Breathability. Unlike CPE, SMS fabric allows air and moisture vapour to pass through, improving wearer comfort during extended procedures. For surgical teams who may wear gowns for two hours or more, this breathability difference is significant.
  • Fluid resistance with weight. SMS gowns are not fully waterproof like CPE, but heavier-weight SMS (45 g/m² and above) provides substantial fluid resistance for most clinical scenarios. The fluid repellency can be further enhanced with chemical treatment during manufacturing.
  • Soft hand feel. The outer spunbond layer is softer and more fabric-like than CPE, making SMS gowns more comfortable for extended wear and reducing the "plastic" feel that some staff find uncomfortable with film-based gowns.

The main trade-off with SMS gowns is cost — they are typically more expensive than CPE and PP gowns due to the additional manufacturing complexity of the three-layer structure. For many facilities, the optimal strategy is to use SMS gowns for surgical and high-risk procedural use, while reserving CPE or PP gowns for lower-risk applications.

PP+PE Gowns — The Hybrid Solution

PP+PE gowns combine a PP nonwoven outer layer with a PE film lamination on the inner side. This hybrid construction gives the gown the soft, fabric-like exterior of PP with the complete liquid impermeability of PE. The result is a gown that feels more comfortable against the skin than pure CPE while offering equivalent waterproof protection.

PP+PE gowns are widely used in:

  • Surgical environments where full liquid barrier is required
  • Emergency department procedures with unpredictable fluid exposure
  • Dental procedures involving aerosol generation and potential splash
  • Cleaning and decontamination tasks where chemical exposure is a factor

Like CPE gowns, PP+PE gowns sacrifice some breathability for protection. However, the PP outer layer is more breathable than CPE's fully chlorinated surface, offering a marginal comfort improvement in warm environments. The lamination also tends to be quieter (less "crinkly") than pure CPE, which some staff prefer.

Matching Gown to Clinical Application

General Nursing and Ward Rounds

For routine patient contact where significant fluid exposure is unlikely, PP nonwoven gowns provide adequate protection with maximum comfort. These are also the appropriate choice for visitors, ancillary staff, and food service personnel who need basic barrier protection without the weight and warmth of waterproof materials.

Wound Care and Dressing Changes

Wound dressing changes can involve unexpected fluid exposure, particularly with surgical wounds, pressure ulcers, and drainage sites. CPE or PP+PE gowns are recommended for these tasks. The complete waterproof barrier ensures that any unexpected fluid contact does not compromise staff protection.

Surgical and Operating Theatre Use

For surgical procedures, SMS gowns are the gold standard. Their combination of microbial barrier performance, breathability for extended wear, and sufficient fluid resistance (at appropriate grammage) makes them ideal for surgical teams. For procedures involving very high fluid volumes — orthopaedic surgery, caesarean section — reinforced SMS or PP+PE gowns with additional front-panel protection may be specified.

Emergency Department and Trauma

Emergency department staff face unpredictable exposure scenarios. CPE gowns are often the default choice here because they provide complete waterproof protection at low cost, and the unpredictability of ED work means that any encounter could involve significant fluid exposure. The higher tear resistance of CPE is also valuable in trauma situations where staff are moving quickly and forcefully.

Dental and Oral Procedures

Dental procedures generate aerosols and can involve splash from water sprays and patient fluids. PP+PE or CPE gowns are appropriate, with the choice depending on procedure duration — PP+PE for longer procedures where comfort matters more, CPE for shorter procedures where maximum barrier is the priority.

Laboratory and Pharmaceutical Use

Laboratory staff handling chemical reagents, biological samples, and hazardous medications benefit from the chemical resistance of CPE gowns. The material's resistance to a wider range of chemicals than PP or SMS makes it the safer choice for these environments. For sterile compounding in pharmacy settings, SMS gowns with appropriate particulate barrier ratings may be specified to meet cleanroom requirements.

After a needlestick incident where a nurse's PP gown tore during an emergency resuscitation, we switched to CPE for all ED and ICU use and SMS for surgery. The cost increase was about 15% overall, but the risk reduction was enormous. You cannot put a price on staff safety." — Infection Control Officer, Tier-2 Hospital, Shandong

Quality Indicators When Sourcing Isolation Gowns

When evaluating isolation gown suppliers, look for the following quality markers:

  • ISO 13485 certification. The manufacturer should operate under a certified quality management system for medical device production, ensuring consistent material quality, process control, and traceability.
  • CE marking. For gowns sold in European and many international markets, CE marking confirms compliance with relevant European standards for protective clothing, including EN 13795 for surgical gowns and drapes.
  • Material grammage specification. Reputable suppliers will specify the grammage (g/m²) of the gown material. For CPE gowns, this typically ranges from 1.5–3.0 g/m² for the film thickness; for PP and SMS, it ranges from 20–55 g/m² depending on the intended use level.
  • Seam construction. The quality of seam sealing — whether heat-sealed, ultrasonically welded, or stitched — affects the overall barrier performance. Heat-sealed or ultrasonically welded seams provide better liquid barrier than stitched seams, which create needle holes that can allow liquid penetration.
  • Closure type and fit. Tie-back closures offer the most adjustable fit for different body sizes. Snap closures are faster to don and doff but may be less secure during active movement. Elastic cuffs help prevent sleeve ride-up and maintain a seal with gloves.
  • Sterilisation options. For surgical use, EO (ethylene oxide) sterilised gowns with individual sterile packaging are available. Standard isolation gowns for non-sterile procedures are supplied non-sterile in bulk packs.

Procurement Strategy — Optimising Your Gown Portfolio

Most healthcare facilities do not need to standardise on a single gown type. In fact, a tiered gown portfolio — matching material to risk level — is typically more cost-effective and safer than a one-size-fits-all approach:

  1. Map gown types to departments and procedures. Work with your infection control team to identify which gown material is appropriate for each department and procedure type. Document this in a gown specification matrix that procurement can follow for ordering.
  2. Track usage by department. Understanding which departments consume which gown types — and at what volumes — enables better forecasting, bulk ordering, and negotiation with suppliers.
  3. Order at carton or pallet level. Isolation gowns are lightweight but bulky, and shipping costs can add up for small orders. Consolidating orders to full-carton or full-pallet quantities typically yields 10–25% savings per unit.
  4. Request samples for clinical evaluation. Before switching suppliers or introducing a new gown type, have clinical staff evaluate samples in real use conditions. Comfort, fit, and ease of donning/doffing affect compliance — a gown that staff find uncomfortable may be used incorrectly or avoided altogether.
  5. Consider OEM branding for distributors. For medical supply distributors, custom-branded isolation gowns with your own label and packaging are available from most manufacturers, including Newrise Medical. This allows you to build a cohesive product line while maintaining supply chain control.

Industry Trends — Where Isolation Gowns Are Heading

The disposable isolation gown market continues to evolve. Several trends are shaping product development and procurement decisions:

  • Sustainability pressure. Healthcare facilities are increasingly scrutinising the environmental impact of single-use PPE. Manufacturers are exploring bio-based nonwoven materials, thinner but higher-performance films, and improved recyclability of gown materials. While full recyclability remains challenging for multi-layer composites, incremental improvements in material efficiency are reducing the waste footprint of each gown.
  • Enhanced comfort standards. Staff feedback is driving demand for gowns that are lighter, more breathable, and less restrictive while maintaining barrier performance. Newer CPE formulations with improved flexibility and SMS fabrics with enhanced breathability reflect this trend.
  • Regulatory harmonisation. International standards for isolation gowns — including AAMI PB70 levels in the United States and EN 13795 in Europe — are becoming more widely referenced in procurement specifications globally. Suppliers who can provide gowns tested and certified against multiple standards are increasingly preferred by international buyers.
  • Smart supply chains. Post-pandemic, healthcare procurement teams are building more resilient supply chains for PPE, including dual-sourcing strategies, safety stock policies, and direct relationships with manufacturers rather than relying solely on distributors. This trend favours manufacturers who can demonstrate production capacity, quality consistency, and reliable delivery schedules.

Conclusion

The "right" isolation gown does not exist — the right gown is the one that matches your clinical need. CPE gowns offer unbeatable waterproof protection at low cost, making them ideal for high-fluid procedures and emergency settings. PP gowns provide maximum comfort and minimum cost for low-risk applications. SMS gowns deliver the best balance of microbial barrier and breathability for surgical use. PP+PE gowns combine fabric-like comfort with complete waterproof protection for demanding clinical scenarios.

The most effective procurement strategy uses all four types in a risk-based allocation system, ensuring that staff are adequately protected without overspending on unnecessary protection.
