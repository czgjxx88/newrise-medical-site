Disposable Suction Catheters: A Complete Guide for Healthcare Procurement

A comprehensive guide to disposable suction catheters for hospitals, clinics, and medical distributors. Compare open and closed suction systems, understand French gauge sizing, evaluate material options and connector standards, and learn procurement best practices for airway management consumables.

Effective airway management is a cornerstone of patient safety across every clinical setting — from emergency departments and operating theatres to intensive care units and general medical wards. At the heart of airway management lies one of the most frequently used yet critically important consumables in modern healthcare: the suction catheter. Designed to remove secretions, blood, vomitus, and other foreign materials from a patient's airway, suction catheters serve as the primary tool for maintaining a patent airway and preventing aspiration-related complications.

Historically, suction catheters were reusable glass or rubber tubes that required thorough cleaning and sterilisation between each use. The shift to single-use disposable suction catheters — driven by infection control best practices and supported by advances in medical-grade polymer extrusion — has dramatically reduced healthcare-associated infection (HAI) risk while improving procedural consistency. Today, disposable suction catheters are available in a wide range of sizes, tip designs, material compositions, and system configurations, making informed product selection an important responsibility for healthcare procurement teams.

The global suction catheter market continues to expand, fuelled by rising surgical volumes, increasing prevalence of respiratory conditions, growing geriatric populations requiring airway support, and ongoing investment in emergency medical services. For hospitals, clinics, and medical supply distributors, understanding the technical specifications, quality indicators, and regulatory requirements surrounding suction catheters is essential for building a reliable, cost-effective supply chain.

Types of Disposable Suction Catheters

Suction catheters are categorised by their design, intended use, and the system configuration in which they operate. Selecting the right type depends on the clinical scenario, patient population, and existing suction equipment infrastructure.

Open Suction Catheters

Open suction catheters are the most common and widely used type across general hospital settings. These catheters are inserted through an endotracheal tube or tracheostomy tube, or used for oral and nasopharyngeal suctioning, and then removed and discarded after a single use. The "open" designation refers to the fact that the patient's airway circuit is interrupted during the procedure — the patient is disconnected from the ventilator circuit (when applicable), the catheter is introduced, suction is applied, and the catheter is withdrawn before reconnection. Open catheters are cost-effective, simple to use, and available in the widest range of sizes and configurations.

Closed Suction Catheters (In-Line Systems)

Closed suction catheters — also called in-line suction catheters — remain connected to the ventilator circuit through a sealed sleeve, allowing suctioning without interrupting mechanical ventilation or opening the airway circuit. The catheter slides through a diaphragm within the sleeve, and after use it is retracted back into the sleeve for potential re-use during the same ventilator episode (typically within a 24-hour window for the same patient). Closed systems offer several advantages: they maintain positive end-expiratory pressure (PEEP), reduce the risk of ventilator-associated pneumonia (VAP) by preserving circuit integrity, and minimise exposure of healthcare workers to aerosolised secretions — particularly important in infectious disease settings. They are especially valuable in intensive care units where patients require prolonged mechanical ventilation.

Yankauer Suction Tips

Yankauer suction tips — often referred to as tonsil suction tips — are rigid, curved-tip suction instruments designed for oral and pharyngeal suctioning. Named after the American otolaryngologist Sidney Yankauer, these tools feature a rounded tip with side and end openings that allow efficient clearance of secretions and blood from the oropharynx without traumatising surrounding tissue. Disposable Yankauer tips are standard equipment in operating rooms, emergency departments, and anaesthesia carts worldwide. They are available with or without a thumb-controlled suction vent, and in both standard and paediatric sizes.

French Gauge Sizing

Suction catheter sizes are specified using the French gauge system (Fr or F), where 1 French equals approximately 0.33 mm in outer diameter. Correct sizing is critical: a catheter that is too large can cause airway trauma, mucosal damage, or complete airway obstruction during insertion, while one that is too small may be ineffective at clearing viscous secretions.

Size (Fr) Outer Diameter (mm) Typical Patient Population
5–6 Fr 1.7–2.0 mm Neonates; extremely low birth weight infants; neonatal ICU suctioning
8 Fr 2.7 mm Infants and small children; paediatric wards; nasopharyngeal suctioning
10 Fr 3.3 mm Older children; smaller adult oral/nasopharyngeal suctioning
12 Fr 4.0 mm Standard adult oral/nasopharyngeal suctioning; routine general ward use
14 Fr 4.7 mm Adult endotracheal suctioning (ETT sizes 7.0–8.0 mm); the most commonly used adult size
16 Fr 5.3 mm Adult endotracheal suctioning with larger ETTs; thick secretion clearance
18 Fr 6.0 mm Large adult airways; thick or tenacious secretions; bronchoscopy-assisted suctioning

A general rule of thumb for endotracheal suctioning is that the catheter's outer diameter should not exceed 50% of the endotracheal tube's inner diameter. This ensures adequate gas flow around the catheter during the suction procedure and prevents barotrauma or atelectasis caused by excessive airway obstruction.

Procurement tip: Stock at least three sizes per patient population (e.g., 8 Fr, 10 Fr, and 12 Fr for paediatric; 12 Fr, 14 Fr, and 16 Fr for adult) to accommodate anatomical variation and different clinical scenarios. Standardising on a consistent brand reduces staff confusion and improves procedural speed.

Material Selection

Disposable suction catheters are manufactured from several medical-grade polymers, each offering distinct advantages depending on the clinical application.

Material Key Properties Best For
Polyvinyl Chloride (PVC) Flexible; cost-effective; clear transparency; widely available; compatible with standard suction connectors General-purpose suctioning; high-volume standard care; most common material for open suction catheters
Silicone-coated PVC Reduced friction; smoother insertion; improved mucosal comfort; slightly higher cost than plain PVC Sensitive airways; paediatric suctioning; repeated suctioning episodes in ICU patients
Thermoplastic elastomer (TPE) Soft yet firm; biocompatible; latex-free; good memory; consistent wall thickness Premium catheter lines; closed suction systems; facilities prioritising material safety and performance
Medical-grade polypropylene (PP) Rigid; used for Yankauer and rigid suction tips; chemical-resistant; autoclave-stable (though typically single-use) Yankauer suction tips; rigid oral suction instruments; operating room and anaesthesia applications

PVC remains the dominant material for flexible open suction catheters due to its favourable combination of flexibility, cost, and manufacturability. However, facilities with high volumes of repeated suctioning procedures — particularly in intensive care — often prefer silicone-coated or TPE catheters for their reduced insertion trauma and improved patient comfort. For Yankauer tips, polypropylene is the standard material, providing the rigidity needed for effective oral clearance without bending or kinking.

Connector Standards and Compatibility

Suction catheter connector compatibility is critical for safe and efficient clinical use. Most suction catheters use a standard 22 mm conical connector at the proximal end, compatible with standard vacuum suction tubing. The distal (patient) end features eyelets — small openings along the catheter shaft — through which secretions are drawn. Eyelet design directly impacts suction efficiency and mucosal safety.

Eyelet Configuration Description Clinical Implication
Single end opening One opening at the catheter tip Simple and cost-effective; higher risk of mucosal suction (trauma); adequate for general use
Two side eyelets (bilateral) Two openings on opposite sides near the tip Reduced mucosal trauma; more even suction distribution; better for repeated or sensitive procedures
Two side eyelets plus end opening (tri-eye) Two bilateral eyelets plus one at the tip Maximum suction efficiency; optimal for thick secretions; most versatile configuration

Closed suction systems use proprietary connector interfaces designed to mate with specific ventilator circuits. When procuring closed suction catheters, compatibility with the facility's existing ventilator brands and circuit configurations must be verified. Many manufacturers offer universal adapters or multi-circuit compatible systems to address this challenge.

Key Design Features to Evaluate

Depth Markings

Quality disposable suction catheters include printed depth markings at regular intervals (typically every 5 cm) along the catheter shaft. These markings enable the clinician to control insertion depth precisely, reducing the risk of carinal stimulation, bronchial trauma, or incomplete clearance. Markings should be legible, durable, and resistant to rubbing off when the catheter is handled with gloved hands or exposed to fluids.

Thumb-Controlled Suction Vent

Most open suction catheters include a thumb-controlled vent (sometimes called a whistle-tip vent) at the proximal end. This vent allows the operator to control suction application — blocking the vent creates vacuum at the distal eyelets, while releasing it stops suction. The vent mechanism should be responsive, easy to operate with gloved hands, and reliable throughout the procedure. Poor-quality vent designs that leak or stick can compromise suction effectiveness and prolong the procedure.

Connector Cap (Closed Systems)

Closed suction catheter systems include a cap or seal at the proximal end that maintains circuit pressure when the catheter is retracted. The cap should seal reliably, be easy to open and close with gloved hands, and maintain integrity throughout repeated catheter insertions and retractions during the same patient episode.

Quality Evaluation Checklist

When assessing suction catheter suppliers, evaluate product quality using the following criteria:

  • Wall thickness consistency: Uniform wall thickness prevents kinking during insertion and ensures consistent suction flow. Visually inspect and manually flex samples to check for thin spots or irregularities.
  • Eyelet smoothness: Eyelet edges should be smooth and free of burrs or sharp moulding edges that could traumatise airway mucosa during insertion or suctioning. Run a gloved finger along the eyelet edges to detect any roughness.
  • Connector fit: The 22 mm connector should engage snugly with standard suction tubing without requiring excessive force and without creating an air leak. Test with your facility's existing suction tubing.
  • Thumb vent responsiveness: Block and release the thumb vent several times. It should create and release suction instantaneously with minimal thumb pressure. Vents that require excessive force or fail to seal completely are a safety concern.
  • Depth marking durability: Rub the printed depth markings firmly with a gloved finger. They should not smudge or rub off, as illegible markings defeat their clinical purpose.
  • Catheter flexibility vs. pushability: The catheter should be flexible enough to navigate the airway curvature without trauma, but rigid enough to be advanced without buckling. This balance is particularly important for endotracheal suctioning.
  • Packaging sterility: For sterile catheters, verify that the packaging is intact, properly sealed, and displays lot numbers, expiry dates, and sterilisation method indicators clearly.
Tip for procurement teams: Conduct a blind comparison test with nursing staff. Provide anonymised samples from two or three suppliers and ask experienced respiratory therapists and nurses to rate them on insertion feel, vent responsiveness, eyelet smoothness, and overall preference. Clinical staff feedback often reveals quality differences that specifications alone do not capture.

Regulatory Standards and Compliance

ISO 13485 — Quality Management

Manufacturers operating under an ISO 13485 certified quality management system demonstrate consistent control over raw material qualification, extrusion process parameters, in-process dimensional inspection, finished product testing, batch traceability, and corrective action procedures. For suction catheters, ISO 13485 coverage extends to dimensional tolerances (French gauge accuracy), connector compatibility testing, eyelet placement and smoothness verification, and sterility assurance processes where applicable.

ISO 10993 — Biocompatibility

Suction catheters contact mucosal tissue during use and should comply with relevant ISO 10993 biocompatibility requirements. Testing typically includes cytotoxicity, sensitisation, and irritation assessments for the materials that contact the patient. Procurement specifications should confirm that all patient-contacting materials are latex-free, DEHP-free, and compliant with applicable biocompatibility standards.

Sterility Standards

Endotracheal and tracheal suction catheters are typically supplied sterile (via ethylene oxide or gamma irradiation sterilisation), while oral/nasopharyngeal catheters may be supplied in non-sterile configurations depending on market requirements. Sterile catheters must display sterilisation method, lot number, expiry date, and sterility integrity indicators on individual packaging. Non-sterile catheters should be manufactured under controlled cleanroom conditions to minimise bioburden.

CE Marking and FDA Registration

In the European Union, suction catheters are classified as medical devices under the Medical Device Regulation (MDR) and require appropriate CE marking. In the United States, suction catheters are generally Class I or Class II devices requiring establishment registration and compliance with applicable performance standards. Procurement teams serving regulated markets should verify the manufacturer's registration status and conformity assessment documentation.

Procurement Best Practices

Standardise Where Possible

Maintaining a standardised suction catheter portfolio — limited to one or two trusted brands per size and type — reduces staff training requirements, minimises procedural errors, and improves inventory management. Work with clinical stakeholders (respiratory therapy, nursing, anaesthesia) to agree on a standard range before placing volume orders. Avoid unnecessary product variation that creates confusion during emergencies.

Consider Total Cost of Ownership

The per-unit cost of a suction catheter is only one component of total cost. Consider insertion success rate (catheters that kink or fail mid-procedure must be replaced, doubling the per-use cost), staff time (easier-to-use catheters reduce procedure time and associated labour costs), complication rates (poor-quality catheters can cause mucosal trauma, bleeding, or infection — generating downstream treatment costs), and packaging efficiency (compact, well-designed packaging reduces storage footprint and shipping costs per unit).

Maintain Safety Stock

Suction catheters are essential airway management consumables. Supply interruptions can directly impact patient safety. Maintain a minimum of 4–6 weeks of safety stock for critical sizes (14 Fr and 16 Fr for adult, 8 Fr and 10 Fr for paediatric), and establish relationships with at least two qualified suppliers to mitigate supply chain risk.

Storage and Handling

Store suction catheters in their original packaging in a clean, dry environment away from direct sunlight, extreme temperatures, and chemical vapours. Sterile catheters should be stored with attention to the manufacturer's specified shelf life and handled in a manner that preserves packaging integrity. Implement FIFO (first in, first out) stock rotation to ensure older stock is consumed first. For closed suction systems, verify compatibility with existing ventilator inventory before committing to volume orders, as circuit interfaces are often brand-specific.

Environmental Considerations

As with all single-use medical consumables, disposable suction catheters generate plastic waste. The majority of flexible catheters are made from PVC, which is technically recyclable but typically disposed of through clinical waste streams due to biohazardous contamination. Procurement teams can reduce environmental impact by selecting manufacturers who minimise packaging materials, use recyclable or bio-based outer packaging, and implement lean manufacturing practices that reduce production waste. The industry is exploring alternative polymer formulations and closed-loop recycling programs for uncontaminated packaging materials, though these solutions remain limited for biohazardous clinical waste.
